6  Probability

Two oversized dice rolling into view and settling on a three and a four.

In the previous chapter, we hinted at thinking probabilistically about extreme scores in a distribution. Here, we’ll go into that idea in more detail. We’ll think about what statisticians mean when they talk about probability and how we go about quantifying it. We’ll need to introduce some new terminology, including probability distributions, and then we’ll return to \(z\) scores and see how a normal distribution lets us attach precise probabilities to regions of scores.

A Brief History of Probability

People have been thinking about probability for a long time, at least in a broad sense. It’s a useful thing to be able to make predictions about an uncertain future. Can I kill this sabre-toothed tiger, or will it probably kill me first? How much crop should we plant to feed the group? For most of that history, there were no formal ways of articulating how probable something might be. The best people could do was offer rough verbal descriptions. Aristotle distinguished between things that are pretty much unpredictable (“the accidental”), certainties (“that which is always”), and “that which is for the most part”—things that happen with some predictability. “That honey-water is useful for a patient in a fever is true for the most part,” Aristotle wrote as an example (Aristotle, n.d., Book VI, sec. 2). Sometimes it might help, sometimes not: it’s not certain, but neither is it entirely unpredictable. It’s something in between, more or less probable.

But how much more or less? The idea of representing uncertainty with numbers and reasoning about it mathematically was a relatively recent development. One of the early inspirations came not from life-and-death decisions but from games of chance. For thousands of years people have been playing games using bits of animal bone — two-sided, coin-like worked and decorated pieces of bone, or astragali, the four-sided ankle bones of sheep and goats. Games played this way were so popular that some people even manufactured astragali out of clay, stone or glass.

According to the card describing this glass astragal at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, playing with them was so much fun that “knucklebones have been found in tombs where they must have been intended to help the deceased while away endless time.” (So I guess, in a way, it was a matter of life and death.)

Astragali have four faces stable enough to land on, but the faces aren’t the same size or shape, so the four results aren’t equally likely. Cubical dice offered a more regular alternative to the irregular animal bones. The example below, something like 4,000 years old, even has the convention of opposite sides adding to seven that modern dice follow. These regularly shaped dice proved appealing to gamblers, and eventually to mathematicians, because it meant they could count outcomes and reason about them in a consistent way.

Catan

To see how this kind of probabilistic thinking works, let’s think about a relatively recent game, Catan. It’s a board game played by rolling a pair of dice (not goat knucklebones, thankfully). The board consists of hexagonal terrain tiles, each producing a resource such as wood or grain. Resources are good, so you want to get as many as possible. (We’ll ignore the differences between resources and other complications of the game.)

Most tiles have a number token. Players place settlements at the corners where tiles meet, and every time the dice are rolled, a settlement gets a resource from each neighboring tile whose number matches the sum of the two dice. So choosing where to settle means choosing which sums will pay you for the rest of the game. Suppose you get to go first, and you just want the greatest chance of getting something on each roll. Where would you settle? Why?

Defining Probability

Let’s lay out some technical terms now, so that we have the precise language we need to reason about whether you picked the best spot.

First, what exactly do we mean by probability? As you might expect, there are distinct schools of thought and intense disagreement, which I am happy to sidestep for the most part. In this book, we will take an approach based in the frequentist tradition, since it’s the one that has dominated the social sciences for most of their history. A probability, in the frequentist sense, is a number that describes what would happen if we could repeat something many times: the proportion of repetitions in which some event of interest would occur in the long run. Probabilities range from 0 to 1. A probability of .5 means that, in the long run, we would expect the event to occur about half the time.

Let’s think about some simple situations in which there are a small number of equally-likely outcomes that we can count. (Later, we’ll generalize the same ideas to situations where outcomes are not equally likely, and to continuous variables and models where counting individual outcomes no longer works.) We’ll need some terms:

  • An outcome is one elementary result of a chance process.
  • An event is the outcome or collection of outcomes we’re actually interested in.
  • The sample space is the set of all the possible outcomes.

We use a capital letter \(P\) to denote probability and put the event we’re interested in inside parentheses. So \(P(A)\) means the probability of Event A. When every outcome in the sample space is equally likely, we can find that probability by counting the outcomes in A and dividing by the total number of possible outcomes.

\[ P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}} \]

If you flip a fair coin, for example, there are two possible outcomes, heads or tails. The entire sample space consists of those two outcomes. If we want to denote the probability of heads, one of those two equally likely outcomes, we would write \(P(\text{heads}) = 1/2 = .5\). (Effectively the event is the outcome; there’s only one outcome that gets you heads.) Likewise, we would write the probability of tails as \(P(\text{tails}) = 1/2 = .5\).

Similarly, if we roll a fair six-sided die, there are six equally likely outcomes in the sample space, the die’s faces, one through six. So the probability of rolling a six is \(P(6) = 1/6 \approx .17\).

Perhaps we’re interested in the event of rolling an even number on a single six-sided die. Now we have an event that can be produced by more than one outcome. There are three individual outcomes (2, 4, 6) that would constitute that event. So the probability of rolling an even number is \(P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 1/2 = .5\).

Rolling Two Dice

The same logic applies to rolling multiple dice; the sample space just expands, and we have to be careful about how to count things. Something that tripped up some early gamblers is that we’re interested in the sum of the dice, like the probability of rolling a 7, but some sums can be arrived at in multiple ways.1 Did you notice the red and blue dice at the top of the chapter? Red landed on 3 and blue landed on 4, for a sum of 7. But what if red had instead landed on 4 and blue on 3? The total would have been 7 as well. We need to count all the different ways each die might land to give the various possible totals.

Assuming both dice are fair and neither die’s result affects the other’s, each of the six red results can be paired with each of the six blue results, giving \(6 \times 6 = 36\) equally likely outcomes. Table 6.1 shows them all: rows give the red die’s result; columns give the blue die’s result.

We can take the 36 sums in the table and draw a bar graph counting how often each value appears. Each bar in Figure 6.3 counts the entries in the table that give that total. A total of 2 appears only once, for example, while 7 appears six times.

Now let’s make a similar version of the graph, with a small mathematical change:

Did you notice the difference? We divided each count by the total, 36, to turn frequencies into proportions. The shape of the distribution is the same; only the vertical scale changes.

It’s a small mathematical tweak but a bigger conceptual leap. Rather than counting every outcome in the sample space once, as in Figure 6.3, each bar tells us the long-run proportion of future rolls on which that total would occur. These probabilities form a probability distribution. It shows every possible value of the variable and the probability attached to each one. The counts in Figure 6.3 add up to 36; the probabilities in Figure 6.4 add up to 1.

We can use it to predict frequencies: over 100 rolls, for example, we’d expect a total of 7 about \(100 \times (6/36) \approx 17\) times. (If we actually roll the dice 100 times we might not get exactly that many sevens; we’re describing what to expect from the chance process, not a strict guarantee. More on this a little later.)

One more thing to note. If we’re interested in two or more mutually exclusive events, meaning they cannot happen together on the same roll, we can add their probabilities to find the probability of one or the other. For example, the probability of rolling a sum of 5 or 6 is:

\[ P(5 \text{ or } 6) = P(5) + P(6) = \frac{4}{36} + \frac{5}{36} = \frac{9}{36} \approx .25 \]

Back to Catan

Now we can return to your settlement in Catan. Armed with the tools and terms we just outlined, are you still pleased with your choice?

We can definitively answer the question of which corner is best. On this board it’s where the 5, the 6 and the 9 meet, up near the top. It pays out if the total is any one of those numbers. Since those events are mutually exclusive, we can add:

\[ P(5 \text{ or } 6 \text{ or } 9) = \frac{4 + 5 + 4}{36} = \frac{13}{36} \approx .36 \]

Nothing else on this board gets past \(10/36\), or about .28. The board had some clues: the red numbers, the 6s and 8s, are individually the most likely totals (since there’s no 7 on this board; in the real game something else happens when 7 is rolled). But a settlement’s probability depends on all the different totals that pay it. The little dots on the tokens were another clue: they count how many of the 36 outcomes produce that total. To find the probability of any intersection on this board, add the dots on the tiles it touches and divide by 36.

Of course, just picking the best spot in a statistical sense won’t win you the game. Like we pointed out already, a probability of about \(.36\) does not guarantee that those three numbers will occur on exactly 36 out of 100 real rolls. In a single game of Catan, any given corner might pay out more or less than expected.2 But at least now you can reason about your choice in a precise way.

Probability and Distributions of Scores

The same logic applies to the scores we study in social science. We can ask for the probability of randomly selecting a score equal to a value, above a value, or within a range.

Take this little distribution of ten scores.

If we select one score at random, each of the ten blocks is one equally likely outcome. Let \(X\) stand for the value of the score we select. Four of the ten blocks have a value of 1, so:

\[ P(X = 1) = \frac{4}{10} = .4 \]

There are no blocks at 3, so \(P(X=3)=0\). And we can define events that collect several values at once:

\[ P(X \ge 4) = \frac{3}{10} = .3 \]

\[ P(1 < X < 5) = \frac{5}{10} = .5 \]

The block histogram describes scores we’ve already observed, but those proportions also tell us what to expect when we randomly select one of the blocks. If we repeatedly select a block at random, putting it back each time, we would expect about 40% of our selections to give a score of 1. Once again, we’re using a distribution to reason about selections we haven’t made yet.

What if we’re interested in a continuous variable, such as height or reaction time, rather than a little collection of scores we can count? Remember the smooth curves in Chapter 2. They represented models of how scores were distributed, including scores we hadn’t actually observed. We called the height density back in Chapter 2. A high part of the curve means scores are concentrated around that value, but the density itself isn’t a probability. We scale the curve so that its total area is 1. Computing the area between two scores then gives the probability of a randomly selected score falling within that interval.3

For the curve in Figure 6.7, which you might recognize from Chapter 2, about 30% of its area lies between 2 and 3. That means a randomly selected score from that modeled distribution has about a .30 probability of falling within that range. Of course, the math behind that answer depends on the precise contours of that curve: a different shape would produce different probabilities. That’s why I mentioned back in Chapter 2 that one curve in particular would become very important to us: the normal curve.

The Normal Distribution

The normal distribution is so useful to us because its entire shape is determined by just two numbers: the mean and the standard deviation. Those two features are enough to determine probabilities under a normal model.

Okay, the math that defines the curve is more involved than I’m making it sound. Here’s the equation:

\[Y = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(X-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \tag{6.1}\]

You don’t really have to know this equation for the work we’re going to do throughout this book, so if it makes you sweat just looking at it, don’t worry. But it is worth looking at briefly to get a sense of how it’s constructed. The value \(Y\) gives the height of the curve (its density) at a particular value of \(X\) (which is part of the exponent over to the right). You can find both the parameters, \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), along with some other constants like \(e\) and \(\pi\). But again, don’t worry about remembering the specifics. Just know that the equation defines the curve precisely, its shape is entirely determined by the mean and standard deviation, and it gives us a curve with a total area of 1, so we can read its areas as probabilities in exactly the way we just described.

Continuing with the historical theme of games of chance, this was figured out in large part by French mathematician Abraham de Moivre, while thinking about the long-run probabilities of simple events with two equally-likely outcomes, like a coin toss. Like we saw earlier, for a fair coin, each outcome (heads or tails) has a probability of \(.5\). But what happens when you toss a coin a bunch of times, say 3,600? Will you get precisely half—1,800—come up heads? Probably not. Toss the coin another 3,600 times and you’ll get a different answer again. If you had the time to do this over and over again, the answers would usually be somewhere close to the expected 1,800; occasionally (but rarely) would it be far away.

De Moivre realized that we can approximate the distribution of all these possible outcomes with a simple smooth curve—the normal distribution. He calculated that the distribution was centered at 1,800 and had a spread equivalent to what we would now call a standard deviation of 30. Using his normal curve, De Moivre worked out that if we were to flip a coin 3,600 times, there was about a 68% chance of getting between 1,770 and 1,830 heads (Moivre, 1738, Corollary 4). In other words, .68 of the area under the curve is in the region between those values.

The Unit Normal Table

This math works for a normal distribution with any mean and standard deviation, but it is often easiest to work with the standard normal distribution, also called the unit normal distribution. This is a normal distribution with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Sound familiar? We described standardizing distributions using \(z\)-scores in exactly this way in Chapter 5. For a normally distributed variable, that transformation gives us this standard normal distribution.

So now we can determine areas under the curve and interpret them as the probability of a randomly selected score falling in that region. So, for example, 34.13% of the area of the curve lies between the mean and one standard deviation above the mean. Because it’s symmetrical, the same 34.13% lies between the mean and one standard deviation below the mean. Another 13.59% lies between one and two standard deviations from the mean on each side. And 2.28% lies more than two standard deviations above the mean, with another 2.28% more than two standard deviations below it. Together, the two extreme regions contain about 4.56% of the distribution.

Of course, we aren’t limited to whole-number \(z\)-scores. We can take any \(z\)-score and determine the probability of a score falling above or below that value under a standard normal model. In the olden days, you would look up something called a \(z\) table, a standard normal table, or a unit normal table. (Computers have made these tables largely obsolete, but it’s useful for us to see how they work, because whatever statistical software you might use in practice is applying the same logic and math.) These tables vary in how they’re arranged. The one below lists nonnegative \(z\) scores along with the areas to their left, to their right, and between the mean and the score.

So for a \(z\) score of zero, it’s exactly half and half; 50 percent of the distribution lies on either side of zero, because zero is the mean and the distribution is symmetrical. In other words, under a normal model there is a 50 percent chance of observing a score greater than the mean and a 50 percent chance of observing a score lower than the mean.

If we take a \(z\) score of 1, we look for that row in the table. The area to the left of \(z=1\) is about .84, so roughly 84 percent of the distribution lies below it. In percentile language, a score with \(z=1\) is at about the 84th percentile. The area to the right is just under .16, and the area between the mean (where \(z=0\)) and \(z=1\) is .3413, just like we saw before. How about a \(z\) score of negative one? Standard normal tables don’t always list negative \(z\) scores, because the symmetry of the distribution means the relevant areas are the same as for a positive \(z\) score on the opposite side of the mean. Only the direction flips: the smaller area (0.1587) is to the left of \(z = -1\) and the larger area (0.8413) is to its right.

What if we want the area between two scores, such as \(z=0.5\) and \(z=1.5\)? Subtract the area to the left of \(0.5\) from the area to the left of \(1.5\), leaving just the interval between them:

\[P(0.5 < z < 1.5) = .9332 - .6915 = .2417\]

So about 24.17% of scores in a standard normal distribution fall in that interval.

Applying Probability: Spider-Man

To finish up, let’s turn back to our silly Spider-Man reaction time test from the previous chapter. We found that Peter Parker’s \(z\) score was \(-2.5\), and we said that that seemed pretty extreme. If we model the distribution of reaction times as a normal distribution, as we assumed in Chapter 5, we’re now equipped to be more precise.

What are the chances that if we randomly selected someone from our reference population of ordinary, unbitten people they would have a reaction time as quick as Peter’s or quicker? As you can see on the normal curve, reaction times that fast make up a very small lower-tail region of the distribution.

Our table lists positive \(z\)-scores, so look up \(2.5\) and read the area to its right. By symmetry, this is also the area to the left of \(-2.5\): about .0062, or 0.62%:

\[P(z \le -2.5) \approx .0062\]

In other words, under our assumed model, if we tested a random individual from this population, there’s less than a 1% chance that they would be as quick as or quicker than Peter Parker. That is evidence against the idea that Peter is just an ordinary member of the reference population. It isn’t proof: rare scores do sometimes happen, and this probability is not itself the probability that the spider bite changed him. But it gives us a more precise reason to begin suspecting that something unusual is going on. In Chapter 8, we’ll develop that logic into a formal hypothesis test.

A Note on Normality

One last thing to be clear: the probabilities in the unit normal table only apply to the precise mathematical normal distribution. If the population distribution has a different shape — say, if it’s strongly skewed or bimodal — the normal curve’s areas might not describe it well. Remember that just converting raw scores into \(z\)-scores doesn’t change the shape of the distribution. Standardizing changes the units, but does not turn a nonnormal distribution into a normal one. So in practice we need to assess whether a normal model is reasonable enough for the data we’re working with and for what we’re trying to do with it.

In the next chapter we’ll start examining the distribution of sample statistics, like the mean. And we’ll see that, luckily for us, a distribution of sample means can be approximately normal even when the population of individual scores is not.
