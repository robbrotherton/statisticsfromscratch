6  Probability

Two oversized dice rolling into view and settling on a three and a four.

In the previous chapter, we hinted at thinking probabilistically about extreme scores in a distribution. Here, we’ll go into that idea in more detail. We’ll think about what statisticians mean when they talk about probability and how we go about quantifying it. We’ll need to introduce some new terminology, including probability distributions and independence, and then we’ll return to \(z\) scores and see how a normal distribution lets us attach precise probabilities to regions of scores.

A Brief History of Probability

People have been thinking about probability for a long time, at least in a broad sense. It’s a useful thing to be able to make predictions about an uncertain future. What are the chances it will rain tomorrow? Can I kill this sabre-toothed tiger, or will it probably kill me first? How much crop should we plant to feed the group? For most of that history, there were no formal ways of articulating how probable something might be. The best people could do was offer rough verbal descriptions, like, “this will happen in most cases.” Aristotle pointed out, for example, that if someone has a fever and you give them honey-water, for the most part they will get better. Sometimes they won’t: it’s not certain, but neither is it entirely unpredictable. It’s something in between, more or less probable.

But how much more or less? The idea of representing uncertainty with numbers and reasoning about it mathematically was a relatively recent development. One of the early inspirations came not from life-and-death decisions but from games of chance. For thousands of years people have been playing games using bits of animal bone — two-sided, coin-like worked and decorated pieces of bone, or astragali, the four-sided ankle bones of sheep and goats. Games played this way were so popular that some people even manufactured astragali out of clay, stone or glass.

According to the card describing this glass astragal at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, playing with them was so much fun that “knucklebones have been found in tombs where they must have been intended to help the deceased while away endless time.” (So I guess, in a way, it was a matter of life and death.)

Astragali have four faces stable enough to land on, but the faces aren’t the same size or shape, so the four results aren’t equally likely. Eventually cubical dice replaced the irregular animal bones. The example below, something like 4,000 years old, even has the convention of opposite sides adding to seven that modern dice follow. These regularly shaped dice proved appealing to gamblers, and eventually to mathematicians, because it meant they could count outcomes and reason about them in a consistent way.

Catan

Let’s think about a slightly more recent game, Catan. It’s a board game played by rolling a pair of dice (not goat knucklebones, thankfully). The board consists of hexagonal terrain tiles, each producing a resource such as wood or grain. Resources are good, so you want to get as many as possible. For now, we’ll ignore the differences between them and the other complications of the game.

Most tiles have a number token. Players place settlements at the corners where tiles meet, and every time the dice are rolled, a settlement gets a resource from each neighboring tile whose number matches the total. So choosing where to settle means choosing which dice totals will pay you for the rest of the game. Suppose you get to go first, and you just want the greatest chance of getting something on each roll. Where would you settle? Why?

Defining Probability

Let’s lay out some technical terms now, so that we have the precise language we need to reason about whether you picked the best spot.

First, what exactly do we mean by probability? Generally speaking, a probability model assigns probabilities to events according to a consistent set of rules. What rules? Well, there are distinct traditions and intense disagreement among statisticians and philosophers, which I am happy to sidestep for the most part. The approach that we will take is based in the frequentist tradition. It’s the one that has dominated the social sciences for most of their history, and so it’s the one you ought to understand first.

So a probability, in the frequentist sense, is a number that describes what would happen if we could repeat something many times: the proportion of repetitions in which some event of interest would occur in the long run. Probabilities range from 0 to 1. A probability of .5 means that, in the long run, we would expect the event to occur about half the time.

Let’s think about some simple situations in which there are a small number of equally-likely outcomes that we can count. (Later, we’ll generalize the same ideas to situations where outcomes are not equally likely, and to continuous variables and models where counting individual outcomes no longer works.) We’ll need some terms:

  • An outcome is one elementary result of a chance process.
  • An event is the outcome or collection of outcomes we’re actually interested in.
  • The sample space is the set of all the possible outcomes.

We use a capital letter \(P\) to denote probability and put the event we’re interested in inside parentheses. So \(P(A)\) means the probability of Event A. When every outcome in the sample space is equally likely, we can find that probability by counting the outcomes in A and dividing by the total number of possible outcomes.

\[ P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}} \]

This should make more sense with some concrete examples. If you flip a coin, there are two possible outcomes, heads or tails. The entire sample space consists of those two outcomes. If we want to denote the probability of heads, we would write \(P(\text{heads}) = 1/2 = .5\). That’s because heads is one of the two equally likely outcomes. (Effectively the event is the outcome; there’s only one outcome that gets you heads.) Likewise, we would write the probability of tails as \(P(\text{tails}) = 1/2 = .5\).

Or we can think about rolling a single fair die. If we’re using a six-sided die, there are six equally likely outcomes in the sample space, the numbers one through six. So the probability of rolling a six is \(P(6) = 1/6 \approx .17\).

Perhaps we’re interested in the event of rolling an even number on a single six-sided die. Now we have an event that can be produced by more than one outcome. There are three individual outcomes (2, 4, 6) that would constitute that event. So the probability of rolling an even number is \(P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 1/2 = .5\).

Rolling Two Dice

The same logic applies to rolling multiple dice; the sample space just expands, and we have to be careful about how to count things. Something that tripped up some early gamblers is that we’re interested in the sum of the dice, but some sums can be arrived at in multiple ways.1

Did you notice the red and blue dice at the top of the chapter? Red landed on 3 and blue landed on 4, for a sum of 7. But what if red had instead landed on 4 and blue on 3? The total would have been 7 as well. We need to count all the different ways each die might land to give the various possible totals. Assuming the dice are fair, each of the six red results can be paired with each of the six blue results, giving \(6 \times 6 = 36\) equally likely outcomes. Table 6.1 shows them all: rows give the red die’s result; columns give the blue die’s result.

We can take the 36 sums in the table and draw a bar graph counting how often each value appears, like we did in Chapter 2. Each bar in Figure 6.3 counts the entries in the table that give that total. A total of 2 appears once, while 7 appears six times.

Now let’s make a similar version of the graph, with a small mathematical change:

Did you notice the change? We divided each count by the total, 36, to turn frequencies into proportions. The distribution looks the same; the bars have the same relative heights. Only the vertical scale changes.

Its a small mathematical tweak but a bigger conceptual leap. Rather than counting every outcome in the sample space once, as in Figure 6.3, each proportion tells us the probability of a total on a roll we haven’t made yet. These probabilities form a probability distribution. It shows every possible value of the variable and the probability attached to each one. The original counts add up to 36; the probabilities add up to 1.

We can use it to predict frequencies: over 360 rolls, for example, we’d expect a total of 7 about \(360 \times (6/36)=60\) times. If we actually roll the dice 360 times we might not get exactly 60 sevens, but We haven’t counted any actual sevens to reach that conclusion. And we aren’t promising exactly 60; we’re describing what to expect from the chance process.

One more thing to note. If two events cannot happen on the same trial, they are mutually exclusive, and we can add their probabilities to find the probability of one or the other. For example, the probability of rolling a 5 or a 6 is:

\[ P(5 \text{ or } 6) = P(5) + P(6) = \frac{4}{36} + \frac{5}{36} = \frac{9}{36} \approx .25 \]

Back to Catan

Now we can return to your settlement in Catan. Armed with the tools and terms we just outlined, are you still pleased with your choice?

The best corner on this board is where the 5, the 6 and the 9 meet, up near the top. It pays out if the total is any one of those numbers. Since those events are mutually exclusive, we can add:

\[ P(5 \text{ or } 6 \text{ or } 9) = \frac{4 + 5 + 4}{36} = \frac{13}{36} \approx .36 \]

Nothing else on this board gets past \(10/36\), or about .28. The board had some clues: the red numbers, the 6s and 8s, are individually the most likely totals (since there’s no 7 on this board; in the real game something else happens when 7 is rolled). But a settlement’s probability depends on all the different totals that pay it. The little dots on the tokens were another clue: they count how many of the 36 outcomes produce that total.

Of course, just picking the best spot in a statistical sense won’t win you the game. Like we pointed out already, the mathematical expected frequency of \(.36\) does not guarantee that those three numbers will occur on eaxactly 36 out of 100 real rolls. In a single game of Catan, any given corner might pay out more or less than expected.2 But at least now you can reason about your choice in a precise way.

Counting marbles

Okay, rolling dice is fun, but every statistics textbook I’ve ever read has used the idea of picking marbles out of a jar to illustrate ideas of probability, so it would be remiss of me not to.

Actually, the idea has a long and distinguished pedigree. In The Art of Conjecturing, published in 1713, Jacob Bernoulli asked his reader to imagine an urn containing 5,000 pebbles, in some unknown ratio of white to black. By drawing pebbles repeatedly, someone who couldn’t see the contents could estimate that ratio from the proportion of white pebbles they observed (Bernoulli, 2006).

That observed proportion is an observed relative frequency. Over many independent repetitions of the same chance process, it tends toward the underlying probability: the law of large numbers. This connects the probability we can calculate from known contents with something we can estimate when the contents are hidden. But keeping the chance process the same matters. A small procedural detail, putting the pebble back, turns out to make a difference.

A jar contains 10 white marbles and 40 blue marbles. If each marble is equally likely to be drawn, \(P(\text{white})=10/50=.2\). Now suppose we draw a white marble. Does the next draw still have a .2 probability of being white?

That depends on what we do with the first marble. If we sample with replacement, we put it back and mix the jar before drawing again. There are still 10 white marbles among 50, so the probability stays .2. The draws are independent: knowing the first result doesn’t change the probabilities for the second.

If we sample without replacement, we leave the first marble out. After drawing white, only 9 white marbles and 49 marbles in total remain. The probability of another white is now \(9/49 \approx .184\). The draws are dependent, because knowing the first result changes the probabilities for the second. We can express that with conditional probability notation:

\[P(\text{second white}\mid\text{first white})=\frac{9}{49}.\]

The vertical bar means “given that”: the probability of a second white marble given that the first was white. With replacement, that conditional probability would simply be .2.

In social science, independence has the same meaning: knowing one person’s score doesn’t change the probabilities for another’s. That can fail if, for example, people discuss their answers before responding, or if observations come from members of the same household who share relevant influences. One person needn’t directly cause another’s response for their scores to be related.

A random sample is selected by a chance procedure. Random selection and independence are different ideas: both marble procedures use chance, but only replacing and mixing the marble makes successive draws independent. In the next chapter, we’ll see why independence matters when combining observations into a sample mean, and how this idealization applies when we sample people.

Probability and Distributions of Scores

The same logic applies to the scores we study in social science. We can ask for the probability of randomly selecting a score equal to a value, above a value, or within a range.

Take this little distribution of ten scores.

Block histogram of ten scores: four 1s, three 2s, two 4s, and one 5.

If we select one score at random, each of the ten blocks is one equally likely outcome. Let \(X\) stand for the value of the score we select. Four of the ten blocks have a value of 1, so:

\[ P(X = 1) = \frac{4}{10} = .4 \]

There are no blocks at 3, so \(P(X=3)=0\). And we can define events that collect several values at once:

\[ P(X \ge 4) = \frac{3}{10} = .3 \]

\[ P(1 < X < 5) = \frac{5}{10} = .5 \]

This is the same move we made with the dice: divide each frequency by the total to get a probability distribution. There we divided by 36; here we divide by ten. The block histogram describes scores we’ve already observed, but those proportions also tell us what to expect when we randomly select one of the blocks. For example, \(P(X=1)=.4\) predicts that about 40% of repeated selections, with replacement, would give a score of 1. Once again, we’re using a distribution to reason about selections we haven’t made yet.

The Normal Distribution

What if we’re interested in a continuous variable, such as height or reaction time, rather than a little collection of scores we can count? Remember the smooth curves in Chapter 2. They represented models of how scores were distributed, including scores we hadn’t actually observed. We also met one particular model, the symmetric, bell-shaped normal distribution. Now we can put that curve to work calculating probabilities.

French mathematician Abraham de Moivre discovered the normal curve while thinking about the long-run probabilities of simple events with two equally-likely outcomes, like a coin toss. Like we saw earlier, for a fair coin, each outcome (heads or tails) has a probability of \(.5\). But what happens when you toss a coin a bunch of times, say 3,600? Will you get precisely half—1,800—come up heads? Probably not. Toss the coin another 3,600 times and you’ll get a different answer again. If you had the time to do this again and again, the answers would usually be somewhere close to the expected 1,800; rarely will it be far away. De Moivre realized that we can approximate the distribution that all these outcomes would follow with a simple smooth curve—the bell-shaped curve that we now call the normal distribution.

The normal curve is defined by a mathematical equation:

\[Y = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(X-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \tag{6.1}\]

Now, you don’t really have to know this equation for the work we’re going to do throughout this book, so if it makes you sweat just looking at it, don’t worry. But it is worth looking at briefly to get a sense of how it’s constructed. It has an \((x-\mu)^2\) in there, which should look familiar from our chapter on variability, and \(\sigma\) is part of this too, so variability has something important to do with the shape. The value \(Y\) gives the height of the curve (its density) at a particular value of \(X\). But again, don’t worry about remembering the specifics. Just know that the equation defines the curve precisely.

Here is the connection to probability. With the dice, dividing by 36 gave us probabilities whose total was 1. For a continuous model, we scale the curve so that its total area is 1. The area between two scores then gives the probability of a randomly selected score falling in that interval. If a population can be reasonably modelled by a normal distribution, we can calculate those probabilities without recording and counting every individual score.

This is why we called the height density back in Chapter 2. A high part of the curve means scores are concentrated around that value; the height itself isn’t a probability. To get a probability, we need a region with some width: the area under the curve across the interval we’re interested in. An exact point has no width and therefore no area, so under a continuous model \(P(X=x)=0\). Our recorded measurements are rounded, and a recorded value represents a small interval. Including or excluding a single exact endpoint doesn’t change a continuous-model probability: \(P(X<a)=P(X\le a)\).

A normal distribution is completely specified by its mean and standard deviation. Those can be whatever, but it is often easiest to work with the standard normal distribution, also called the unit normal distribution. This is a normal distribution with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Sound familiar? The \(z\) scores we learned to calculate in the previous chapter put scores onto that standard scale:

\[z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}.\]

If \(X\) follows a normal distribution, its standardized scores follow the standard normal distribution. Standardizing changes the units, but preserves the proportion of scores in each corresponding region. It does not turn a nonnormal distribution into a normal one. We use capital \(Z\) for the standardized variable and lowercase \(z\) for a particular value.

The total area under the curve is 1. The proportion of that area in a region is the probability of a randomly selected score falling in that region, according to the model. So, for example, 34.13% of the area of the curve lies between the mean and one standard deviation above the mean. And because it’s symmetrical, the same 34.13% lies between the mean and one standard deviation below the mean. Another 13.59% lies between one and two standard deviations from the mean on each side. And 2.28% lies more than two standard deviations above the mean, with another 2.28% more than two standard deviations below it. Together, the two extreme regions contain about 4.56% of the distribution.

Standard normal curve divided at z = -2, -1, 0, 1, and 2, with each region labeled by the percentage of the distribution it contains: 2.28%, 13.59%, 34.13%, 34.13%, 13.59%, and 2.28%.

The Unit Normal Table

Of course, we aren’t limited to those particular regions. We can take any \(z\) score and determine the probability of a score falling above or below that value under a standard normal model. In the olden days, you would look up something called a \(z\) table, a standard normal table, or a unit normal table. These tables vary in how they’re arranged. The one below lists nonnegative \(z\) scores along with the areas to their left, to their right, and between the mean and the score. (Computers have made these tables largely obsolete, but it’s useful for us to see how they work, because whatever statistical software you might use in practice is applying the same logic and math.)

So for a \(z\) score of zero, it’s exactly half and half; 50 percent of the distribution lies on either side of zero, because zero is the mean and the distribution is symmetrical. In other words, under a normal model there is a 50 percent chance of observing a score greater than the mean and a 50 percent chance of observing a score lower than the mean.

If we take a \(z\) score of 1, we look for that row in the table. The area to the left of \(z=1\) is about .84, so roughly 84 percent of the distribution lies below it. In percentile language, a score with \(z=1\) is at about the 84th percentile. The area to the right is just under .16, and the area between the mean (where \(z=0\)) and \(z=1\) is .3413, just like we saw before. How about a \(z\) score of negative one? Standard normal tables don’t always list negative \(z\) scores, because the symmetry of the distribution means the relevant areas are the same as for a positive \(z\) score on the opposite side of the mean. Only the direction flips: the smaller area (0.1587) is to the left of \(z = -1\) and the larger area (0.8413) is to its right.

Finding the Area You Need

Start with the event, then identify its region on the curve, and only then choose a table entry. For example, suppose scores follow a normal distribution with \(\mu=100\) and \(\sigma=15\). A score of 115 has \(z=(115-100)/15=1\); a score of 130 has \(z=2\).

Question Standardized event Area from the table
Below 115? \(P(Z<1)\) Left of 1: \(.8413\)
Above 115? \(P(Z>1)\) Right of 1: \(1-.8413=.1587\)
Between 115 and 130? \(P(1<Z<2)\) Left of 2 minus left of 1: \(.9772-.8413=.1359\)
Between 85 and 115? \(P(-1<Z<1)\) Twice the mean-to-1 area: \(2(.3413)=.6826\)

Subtracting the two left-hand areas in the third row removes all the scores below 115, leaving only the region between 115 and 130. In the last row, symmetry gives us equal areas on the two sides of the mean. Each calculation is a way of collecting exactly the region named in the question.

Applying Probability: Spider-Man

To finish up, let’s turn back to our silly Spider-Man reaction time test from the previous chapter. We found that Peter Parker’s \(z\) score was \(-2.5\), and we said that that seemed pretty extreme. But now we’re equipped to determine probabilities under a normal model, and we can be more precise.

What are the chances that if we randomly selected someone from our reference population of ordinary, unbitten people they would have a reaction time as quick as Peter’s or quicker?3 As you can see on the normal curve, reaction times that fast make up a very small lower-tail region of the distribution.

Our table lists positive \(z\) scores, so look up \(2.5\) and read the area to its right. By symmetry, this is also the area to the left of \(-2.5\): about .0062, or 0.62%:

\[P(Z \le -2.5) \approx .0062\]

In other words, if we tested a random individual from this population, there’s less than a 1% chance that they would be as quick as or quicker than Peter Parker. That is evidence against the idea that Peter is just an ordinary member of the reference population. It isn’t proof: rare scores do sometimes happen, and this probability is not itself the probability that the spider bite changed him. But it gives us a more precise reason to begin suspecting that something unusual is going on. In Chapter 8, we’ll develop that logic into a formal hypothesis test.

A Note on Normality

One last thing to note: these particular probabilities come from a normal model. If the population distribution has a different shape — say, if it’s strongly skewed or bimodal — the normal curve’s areas might not describe it well. So in practice we don’t simply assume that every distribution is normal, and we can’t conclusively “verify” normality from a finite set of observations. We assess whether a normal model is reasonable enough for what we’re trying to do.

The objects have changed over the course of the chapter — dice outcomes, marbles, blocks in a histogram, areas under a curve — but the underlying question has stayed the same. What are the possible results, and how much of that possibility belongs to the event we care about? In the next chapter, we’ll ask that question about sample means. And we’ll see an important complication: under certain conditions, a distribution of sample means can be approximately normal even when the population of individual scores is not.
