7  Sampling

Animated sampling distribution cover figure.

In the previous chapter, we used the normal distribution to determine the probability of seeing individual scores within a given range. But researchers usually collect a sample and summarize its scores with a statistic, like the mean. In Chapter 4, we saw that sample means vary because of sampling error, even though their errors balance out in the long run. Now we’ll ask how widely those means vary, and which results would be surprising.

The Quincunx

Before we proceed, take a moment to enjoy this little toy. (It’s a digital version of a real thing you can buy and keep on your desk to turn to in moments of boredom.)

ActivityThe Quincunx
A Galton board: balls fall through twelve rows of pegs, bouncing left or right at random at each peg, and pile up in bins at the bottom, forming a bell-shaped distribution.

Click Replay to start. Try it a couple of times: what changes, and what stays similar?

It’s called a Galton board, after Francis Galton, who developed it in the 1870s. (Galton also called it the Quincunx, meaning five points, as in the arrangement of groups of pegs.) The physical version is a little box with a clear front, rows of pegs, and lots of little balls (originally lead shot, like from shotgun pellets; ammunition manufacturers by this time were able to make the precise, uniform spheres that the demonstration requires).

As Galton inscribed on the original device, when set upright on a level table, “the shot will all drop into the funnel, and running thence through its mouth, will pursue devious courses through the harrow and will accumulate in the vertical compartments at the bottom, there affording a representation of the law of dispersion.”

By “law of dispersion,” Galton meant the tendency for the balls to end up in a predictable distribution—this is the same approximation of the normal distribution that Abraham de Moivre had discovered by thinking about a random process like flipping a coin many times. Each individual ball’s course is unpredictable, but the aggregate of all those “devious courses” looks roughly the same each time.1

In our digital board, each ball encounters twelve pegs, with an independent 50/50 chance of bouncing left or right at each one. To end up in the far-left slot, it has to bounce left every time. There is only one way for that to happen, just like there’s only one way to roll two dice and get a total of two. To land in the middle, a ball needs to take six lefts and six rights, and there are many different sequences that produce that mix, just like there are many ways to roll a seven with the pair of dice. We can’t predict any one ball’s path, but we can describe the pattern of possible landing places.

Sampling Error

Our purpose in this chapter is to understand how sampling from a population works—how we can quantify our trust in a sample as a fair representation of the population it came from. The Quincunx is a nice visual analogy for that process. Imagine that each ball is collecting a sample as it falls through the pegs. Each bounce supplies one observation; the final slot is the sample statistic, summarizing all twelve observations.

Let’s make the analogy more concrete with political opinion polling. Say support for Candidate Left and Candidate Right is split exactly 50/50 in the population. If we select twelve people at random, sampling error means we won’t necessarily find six supporters of each. Seven and five wouldn’t be too surprising. Or even 8 and 4. We could even get twelve supporters of Candidate Left, like a Quincunx ball bouncing left twelve times in a row. It’s unlikely, but not impossible. Repeat the poll over and over with different samples of twelve people each time and the results would form a distribution like the pile on the Galton board: results near 50% support would be common, while results near 0% or 100% would be rare.

Which poll would surprise you more?

Imagine two polls drawn at random from our evenly split population. One asks 12 people, the other asks 1,200. Which would surprise you more if three-quarters of its respondents supported Candidate Left? Why? Try reasoning it out without calculating a probability.

Answer

The poll of 1,200. In a small sample, a few people can tip the proportion a long way from 50/50. We’d expect a larger sample to come closer to the population’s even split, so a result of 75% would be more surprising.

Explanation

Neither result is impossible. The question is how much room chance has to produce a lop-sided result when the population itself is evenly split.

What if we had a sample of 100 people, rather than 12? In this case we’d expect something like 50 people to support each candidate, but it wouldn’t be too surprising if we saw something like 55/45. It would be surprising if we happened to get 100 people who all support Candidate Left. Now what if our sample size was 1,000? Or 1,000,000? Your intuition is probably telling you that the larger the sample, the more surprising an extremely lop-sided result would be.

This is, in effect, what the error bars on graphs of opinion poll results convey. (The precise math behind the margin of error is something we’ll talk more about in Chapter 10.) Figure 7.2 shows the results of two different polls conducted shortly before the 2000 U.S. presidential election, showing people’s support for the two main candidates, George W. Bush and Al Gore (support for other candidates was also surveyed, but their shares are not shown here). The error bars express uncertainty about population support around each estimate, allowing for sampling error.

Notice that the two polls put different candidates ahead, but the gaps are small compared with the uncertainty around each estimate. As Pew put it, “the contest is so close that we cannot confidently predict the winner of the popular vote.” The actual popular vote was 47.9% for Bush and 48.4% for Gore, both within these polls’ margins of error. Sampling variability and people changing their minds or withholding opinions complicate what a poll can tell us.

Sampling Error with IQ Scores

The opinion poll results summarize categorical responses with a proportion. Now we’ll turn to quantitative data. What would happen if we could repeatedly take samples from a population and summarize each sample with a quantitative statistic like the mean?

Say we have a population with normally distributed IQ scores, a mean of 100, and a standard deviation of 15. We randomly collect ten observations, find their mean, then draw another ten and repeat. We did this kind of thing in Activity 4.3 in Chapter 4. There, we tracked the samples’ errors and saw their average approach zero. This time we’ll collect the means themselves into a block histogram, to see which occur most often and how widely they vary as they pile up.

Now we just keep repeating the process. Each sample converges into a blue mean block, which joins the growing pile. After thirty samples, there isn’t much of a pattern. But thirty isn’t very many in the grand scheme of things: we need more.

Let’s speed things up a bit. Each tick we get a new sample of 10 individual scores, from which we compute a new sample mean. After collecting 200 samples, a pattern is becoming evident. The distribution of means piles up around the true population mean in a roughly symmetrical distribution. Most sample means are near 100, and extreme ones are rare. The spread of the distribution of means is less than that of the original population.

Notice how similar the final shape of the histogram of sample means in Activity 7.1 is to the shape of the balls at the bottom of the Quincunx. The process is different; instead of balls randomly bouncing off pegs, we are randomly selecting members of the population. But the reason for the outcome is similar: when we sample randomly from a population, there are lots of ways for a sample mean to be close to the true population mean, but few ways for it to be very far away.

The name for the kind of distribution we created is a distribution of sample means, or sampling distribution of the mean. And the predictability of its shape is the key insight that powers the inferential statistics we’ll begin using in the next chapter.

The Distribution of Sample Means

Technically the sampling distribution of the mean is the distribution of sample means obtained by selecting all possible samples of a given sample size \(n\) from a population. For the IQ distribution, we ended with 200 random samples, not every possible sample. As you might imagine, for any reasonably large population, there’s often a huge number of possible samples; for practical purposes, the number might be effectively infinite. We’ll return to this problem shortly, but we won’t let it stop us from demonstrating the fundamental principles now. We just need a small, manageable population, from which we can obtain all possible samples of a given size. Like, very small.

Suppose we have a population consisting of just four people. We’re interested in their height. We have one person 60 inches tall, one 62, one 64, and one 66.

We can describe some features of this population. Its mean is \(\mu = 63\) inches3, and its standard deviation is \(\sigma = 2.24\) inches.4

But remember, we’re not interested in individuals anymore. We’re interested in samples. So now we’re going to list every possible sample where \(n = 2\), meaning we have two observations in each sample. Table 7.2 lists every possible sample. \(X_1\) is the first score in the sample. \(X_2\) is the second score. And \(M\) is that sample’s mean. Note that for this to work, it is important that we’re sampling independently, meaning that the first observation in each sample does not affect the second. For this tiny population that means the same person can appear twice. The first row, for example, is the 60-inch person twice. Obviously the sample mean there is 60. The next sample consists of the 62-inch person and the 60-inch person, giving a mean of 61. And so on.

In case the tables don’t make this process entirely clear, we can visualize the process of obtaining each sample and its mean step by step.

So for this tiny population, we have been able to exhaustively compile every possible sample mean for samples of size \(n = 2\)—something we could only approximate with the bigger IQ population and the 200 means we collected randomly. But you see the similar pattern? Again, sample means close to the true population mean are relatively common; sample means far from the true population mean are relatively uncommon.

This slightly tedious process has given us a new kind of population: the set of every sample mean. We can describe its characteristics just as we would for any other population.

We can, for example, figure out its mean. The mean of the distribution of sample means is called the expected value of the mean, \(\mu_M\). Its value should be obvious visually. It’s a symmetrical distribution and the peak is right on 63 inches, so that’s the mean. You can also calculate it by adding all 16 sample means and dividing by 16, the number of scores. Remember, 63 was the mean of the population as well, so the mean of the sampling distribution is equal to \(\mu\).

We can also calculate the standard deviation of the 16 means. The standard deviation of the sampling distribution is also called the standard error of the mean. The symbol for this is \(\sigma_M\). We can calculate it in the same way we did in Chapter 4, by putting all those values in a table, working out columns for deviations, the squared deviations, then using those to get the sum of squared deviations, the variance, and the standard deviation. Note that because we’re dealing with a population of sample means we use the population formula for variance, dividing \(SS\) by \(N\) (rather than \(n - 1\) for a sample).

Lastly, because we know every possible mean, we can think about the probability of observing certain sample means from this distribution, just like we thought about the probabilities of obtaining certain scores from a regular population.

The Central Limit Theorem

The trouble with distributions of sample means is that it’s just not feasible in most realistic situations to obtain every possible sample of a given size for a population. Listing every possible sample worked for four people and samples of two. But imagine doing that for, say, all American voters. Even if we knew everyone’s scores, the number of possible samples would be astronomical. (Also, if we knew everyone’s scores, we wouldn’t need to sample anyway.)

Thankfully, some simple equations and a theorem can help. We can predict the sampling distribution’s characteristics without compiling every possible sample. Its center and variability follow from the population mean, population variability, and sample size, and the Central Limit Theorem tells us about its shape.5

Center: The Expected Value of the Mean

The center of the sampling distribution is simple: the average of all the sample means equals the population mean. This is because \(M\) is an unbiased statistic: some of the sample means are above the true population mean, some are below, and they balance out perfectly. So the expected value of the mean is exactly equal to the population mean:

\[\mu_M = \mu \tag{7.1}\]

For our four heights, both the population mean and the mean of all 16 sample means were 63. It wasn’t a coincidence! It reflects the sample mean’s lack of bias.

Variability: The Standard Error of the Mean

Unlike its center, the variability of the sampling distribution (its standard error, \(\sigma_M\)) is not equal to the population variability. Instead it is smaller, as a function of the sample size, \(n\).

\[\sigma_M = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{7.2}\]

The numerator is the population standard deviation, \(\sigma\). So the larger the variability in the population, the larger the standard error of the mean. The denominator is the square root of the sample size. And so the larger our sample size, the smaller the standard error of the mean.

An algebraically equivalent form is useful if you have variance, \(\sigma^2\), rather than the standard deviation, \(\sigma\):

\[\sigma_M = \dfrac{\sqrt{\sigma^2}}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{7.3}\]

You can see how they are equivalent: \(\sigma^2\) is literally just \(\sigma\) squared, so \(\sqrt{\sigma^2}\) is the same as \(\sigma\).

Returning to our toy population of heights, we can figure out the standard error of the mean using just known population characteristics. Remember, we said the standard deviation of the population of four heights is \(\sigma = 2.24\). So if we put it into that equation, we get:

\[\sigma_M = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2.24}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 1.58\]

It’s the same answer as when we went through the full rigmarole of compiling all possible samples for that little population and computing their standard deviation manually! Plugging two numbers into an equation could have saved us a lot of work there.

Of course, the real value is applying the equation where we couldn’t compile all possible samples to double-check the answer. Let’s return to IQ scores and see how sample size affects the standard error.

Shape of the Sampling Distribution

It wasn’t a coincidence that the distribution of the Quincunx balls, IQ samples, and height sampling distribution all took on the same basic shape: approximately normal, with most values clustered in the middle with extreme values trailing off symmetrically on either side. (Okay, the heights sampling distribution was more of a triangle, because of the tiny population and sample size, but still in the same ballpark.) The Central Limit Theorem helps explain when we can expect a sampling distribution to be approximately normal.

If the population we’re sampling from is normally distributed, we’re good: its distribution of sample means will be normal, whatever the sample size. But even if the population is not normal, the distribution of means approaches normality as sample size increases. You’ll sometimes hear \(n = 30\) as a rule of thumb for when the distribution of means is “close enough” to normal. But there’s no magic threshold. For a nonnormal population, the distribution of means never becomes exactly normal; the approximation improves gradually, at a rate that depends on the population’s shape. Activity 7.2 lets us see that in action.

Sampling People in Practice

So now we’ve seen a few examples of normality and predictability emerging from random sampling. We’re almost ready to apply these tools in the context of social science research. But first, let’s take a moment to consider how closely collecting data from people resembles the sampling in our demonstrations. The version of the Central Limit Theorem we’ve used assumes independent observations drawn from the same population distribution; applying it in practice requires those assumptions to be reasonable approximations.

Random Selection, Replacement, and Independence

First let’s define three terms. Random sampling is about how we choose observations: we use a chance procedure. Replacement is about whether someone or something already selected can be selected again within the same sample. Independence is about whether knowing one observation changes the probabilities for another.

In the demonstrations above, I sampled with replacement. This was most obvious with the small population of four heights: the 16 possible samples of \(n = 2\) included samples in which the same person appeared twice. To see why that mattered, let’s turn to another statistics textbook favorite: picking marbles out of a jar.

In The Art of Conjecturing, published in 1713, Jacob Bernoulli asked his reader to imagine an urn containing 5,000 pebbles, in some unknown ratio of white to black. By drawing pebbles repeatedly, someone who couldn’t see the contents could estimate that ratio from the proportion of white pebbles they observed (Bernoulli, 2006).

To keep things simple, imagine a jar with just 50 marbles, 40 blue and 10 white. We want to pick out two marbles, one after the other. If each marble is equally likely to be drawn, the probability of white on our first draw is \(P(\text{white})=10/50=.2\).

So far so simple. But what is the probability that a second draw will also be white? To answer that, we need to know a small but important procedural detail: did we put the first marble back, or leave it out?

If we sample with replacement, we put it back and mix the jar before drawing again. There are still 10 white marbles among 50, so the probability stays .2, whatever color we drew first. The draws are independent.

If we sample without replacement, we leave the first marble out. After drawing white, only 9 white marbles and 49 marbles in total remain, so the probability of another white is \(9/49 \approx .184\). After drawing blue, it would instead be \(10/49 \approx .204\). The draws are dependent: knowing the first result changes the probabilities for the second.

Both procedures are random, but only the first gives us independent draws. For people, choosing names by lottery from a complete population list works the same way. Without replacement, this produces a simple random sample: every possible set of \(n\) people has the same chance of being selected. Random doesn’t mean choosing whoever happens to be nearby, and it doesn’t require replacement.

Can We Treat People’s Scores as Independent?

Do you sense a potential problem here? Social scientists study people, who aren’t like marbles we can put back into a jar. Social scientists generally collect data from each participant once. Strictly speaking, this makes successive selections dependent, as it is sampling without replacement. But like the (non)normality of the sampling distribution, the problem is a matter of degree. What matters is the fraction sampled across the whole sample, not just the tiny change after the first draw.

If the sample is only a small fraction of the population, removing those people barely changes who is available. Selecting 100 people randomly from a population of 10,000 removes just 1% of the population in total. The resulting dependence is weak enough that treating the observations as independent is a good enough approximation. If we sampled a much bigger chunk of those 10,000 people, say 5,000, sampling without replacement would noticeably reduce the variability of the sampling distribution. The usual formula, \(\sigma_M=\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), would fail to account for that reduction and would therefore overstate the standard error.6

Still, would allowing someone to participate twice—sampling with replacement—be better? Usually not. Dependence can come in other forms. In our height example, sampling with replacement gives independent draws of the heights. But for other kinds of research, participating in a study can change someone: they may learn the task or remember the questions. Selecting them again doesn’t make them naive to the procedure, so their second score would not be independent of their first. In that case, sampling without replacement is better, even if it introduces a small amount of dependence across the sample.

Dependence can also arise among different people. Responses from members of one household may share influences; pupils in one classroom share a teacher and learning environment. An opinion poll sampling whole households could underestimate the standard error if it treated all their responses as independent. Studies that do things like that can still be useful, but their analysis needs to account for dependence. (Sometimes we deliberately measure people before and after a treatment, for example. Those paired responses are related, so we need an analysis that accounts for that, which we’ll see in Chapter 13.)

Which Population Are We Sampling?

Lastly, we should point out that even if we are appropriately ensuring independence in a statistical sense, we still need to be careful that our sample is a fair representation of the population we are trying to make inferences about. Remember Köbel’s proposal in Chapter 3 to define a foot by averaging the feet of sixteen men leaving church? Suppose we wanted to estimate the average foot length of the town’s adult men. A simple random sample could happen to include some unusually large feet. That’s sampling error; across repeated random samples, high and low estimates would balance around those men’s mean.

Now imagine trying Köbel’s procedure outside the locker room of the New York Knicks basketball team. We’d probably get a much larger average foot size. Or imagine lining up my kid’s second-grade class: we’d get a much smaller answer. Both procedures systematically favor feet unlike those of the town’s complete population of adult men: that’s selection bias. Even choosing randomly within those niche groups of basketball players or kids wouldn’t fix it.

Somewhat more subtly, say we recruit people for an opinion poll by randomly dialing phone numbers listed in a database. People who answer and agree to tell us their thoughts may differ from those who don’t, and not everyone in the population might have a number in our database. Those are coverage and nonresponse problems. Alternatively, recruiting whoever walks past, volunteers online, or participates for course credit gives us a convenience sample, rather than a simple random sample of the general population. (Arguably Köbel’s churchgoers were a convenience sample; the men who attended church might not represent the broader population.)

Convenience sampling can be perfectly acceptable for some research questions, and the Central Limit Theorem still applies if treating its scores as independent observations from a common population distribution is reasonable and the sample is large enough. But generalizing beyond the population we actually sampled needs a separate argument, and increasing the sample size can’t fix a procedure that systematically produces biased data.

Summary

So to repeat and summarize, assuming we are independently sampling from a population, the distribution of sample means has an expected value, \(\mu_M\), equal to \(\mu\). It has a standard deviation, or standard error of the mean, of \(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\). Its shape is exactly normal at every \(n\) when the population is normal. For a nonnormal population, its standardized shape approaches normality gradually as \(n\) increases, at a rate that depends on the shape of the population. \(n = 30\) is often a useful convention, but it is not a magical cutoff. And we need to consider whether treating our observations as independent is reasonable, and whether the population we sampled is the one we want to make inferences about. These insights power much of the inferential statistics that we’ll move on to later, because they allow us to reason about the probability of sample statistics rather than only individual scores.

A histogram that more closely follows the predicted sampling distribution.

Explanation

With more sample means, the random bumps in the histogram tend to settle down, revealing the sampling distribution more clearly. But \(n\) is still 5: we have more samples, not more observations in each sample. The population and sample size haven’t changed, so neither have the predicted center, standard error, or shape.

5

Independent observations are drawn from a population with variance \(\sigma^2 = 100\). What is the standard error of the mean for samples of \(n = 25\)?

Hint

Find the population standard deviation before using \(\sigma_M = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\).

Answer

2

Explanation

\(\sigma = \sqrt{100} = 10\), so \(\sigma_M = 10/\sqrt{25} = 2\). The individual scores have a standard deviation of 10; the sample means have a standard deviation of 2.

6

With independent observations from the same population, samples of \(n = 25\) give a standard error of 2. What sample size would reduce the standard error to 1?

Answer

100

Explanation

Halving \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) requires doubling \(\sqrt{n}\), so we need four times as many observations per sample: \(4 \times 25 = 100\). Doubling the sample size would not halve the standard error.

7

We draw samples of five independent observations from a normally distributed population. What is the shape of the distribution of all possible sample means?

Response options

Exactly normal.

Approximately normal, becoming exactly normal at \(n = 30\).

Flat, because every possible mean is equally likely.

Impossible to determine without simulating the samples.

Answer

Exactly normal.

Explanation

When the population is normal, the distribution of means is normal at every sample size. A histogram of only a few simulated means can still look lumpy.

8

Once sample size reaches \(n = 30\), a nonnormal sampling distribution becomes exactly normal.

Response options

True

False

Answer

False

Explanation

The distribution of means becomes closer to normal gradually as sample size increases. Thirty is sometimes a useful rule of thumb, but it isn’t a magical cutoff. How large a sample we need depends on the shape of the population we’re sampling from.

9

A researcher puts every student’s name in a hat, mixes it, and draws 20 different names without putting any back. Which description fits the selection of students?

Response options

Random, with independent selections.

Random, with dependent selections.

Nonrandom, with independent selections.

Nonrandom, with dependent selections.

Answer

Random, with dependent selections.

Explanation

This is a simple random sample without replacement. Each selected name changes who remains available, so the selections are dependent. Replacement allows repeat selections in our toy examples; randomness does not require it.

10

We select a simple random sample from a population without replacement. When can we still use \(\sigma_M = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\) as a useful approximation of the standard error of the mean?

Response options

When the sample includes only a small fraction of the population.

When the sample includes nearly the whole population.

Whenever the sample includes at least 30 observations.

Only when every selected person can participate twice.

Answer

When the sample includes only a small fraction of the population.

Explanation

If we select only a small fraction of the population, removing those people barely changes who is available. We can treat the observations as approximately independent. If we sample most of the population, sampling without replacement reduces how widely the sample means vary, so \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) overstates the standard error.

11

A poll samples whole households. Because every respondent is a different person, it is reasonable to treat all their responses as independent.

Response options

True

False

Answer

False

Explanation

People in the same household may share influences. Treating those related responses as independent can underestimate the standard error. Measuring each person only once doesn’t rule out these shared influences.

12

A researcher wants to estimate how many hours adults sleep on average, but recruits volunteers from an Intro Psych class. Would recruiting more students in the same way fix the problem of selection bias?

Response options

Yes, once the sample includes at least 30 students.

Yes, once the standard error is small enough.

No, the volunteers may still differ from the broader population of adults.

No, a sample can only represent a population if it includes everyone.

Answer

No, the volunteers may still differ from the broader population of adults.

Explanation

A larger sample can have a smaller standard error, but these students may still have different sleeping habits from the broader population of adults. Increasing the sample size can’t fix a procedure that systematically produces biased data.
