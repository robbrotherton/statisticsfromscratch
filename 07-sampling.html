7  Sampling

Animated sampling distribution cover figure.

In the previous chapter, we used the normal distribution to determine the probability of seeing individual scores within a given range. But researchers usually collect a sample and summarize its scores with a statistic, like the mean. In Chapter 4, we saw that sample means vary because of sampling error, even though their errors balance out in the long run. Now we’ll ask how widely those means vary, and which results would be surprising.

The Quincunx

Before we proceed, take a moment to enjoy this little toy. (It’s a digital version of a real thing you can buy and keep on your desk to turn to in moments of boredom.)

ActivityThe Quincunx
A Galton board: balls fall through twelve rows of pegs, bouncing left or right at random at each peg, and pile up in bins at the bottom, forming a bell-shaped distribution.

Click Replay to start. Try it a couple of times: what changes, and what stays similar?

The real version is a little box with a clear front, rows of pegs, and lots of little balls (originally lead shot). It’s called a Quincunx (meaning five points, as in the arrangement of the pegs), or a Galton board, after Francis Galton, who developed it in the 1870s.

As Galton inscribed on the original device, when set upright on a level table, “the shot will all drop into the funnel, and running thence through its mouth, will pursue devious courses through the harrow and will accumulate in the vertical compartments at the bottom, there affording a representation of the law of dispersion.”

Those “devious courses” produce surprisingly consistent results. In our digital board, each ball encounters twelve pegs, with an independent 50/50 chance of bouncing left or right at each one. To end up in the far-left slot, it has to bounce left every time. There is only one way for that to happen, just like there’s only one way to roll a total of two with two dice. To land in the middle, it needs six lefts and six rights, and there are many different sequences that produce that mix, just like there are many ways to roll a seven. We can’t predict any one ball’s path, but we can describe the pattern of possible landing places.1

Sampling Error

Our purpose in this chapter is to understand how sampling from a population works—how we can quantify our trust in a sample as a fair representation of the population it came from. The Quincunx is a nice visual metaphor for that process. Imagine that each ball is collecting a sample as it falls. Each bounce supplies one observation; the final slot is the sample statistic, summarizing all twelve observations.

Let’s make an analogy to political opinion polling. Say two candidates are running for office, candidate Left and candidate Right. In reality, support for the two candidates is exactly split, 50/50. If we select twelve people at random and ask which candidate they prefer, we might expect to find that half of the people in our sample support each candidate. But because of the kind of sampling error we encountered in Chapter 4, the proportion in our sample need not be exactly 50/50. It wouldn’t be too surprising if we ended up with seven supporters of Candidate Left and only five of Candidate Right. Or even 8 and 4. In fact, there is a chance that we could randomly end up with 12 people who all support Candidate Left. It’s relatively unlikely, but not out of the question. This is like one of the Quincunx balls bouncing left twelve times in a row and ending up in the far-left slot. Repeat the poll over and over, keeping its size at twelve, and the results would form a distribution like the pile on the board. Results near 50% would be common, while results near 0% or 100% would be rare.

What if we had a sample of 100 people, rather than 12? In this case we’d expect something like 50 people to support each candidate, but it wouldn’t be too surprising if we saw something like 60/40. It would be surprising if we happened to get 100 people who all support Candidate Left. Now what if our sample size was 1,000? Or 1,000,000? Your intuition is probably telling you that the larger the sample, the more surprising an extremely lop-sided result would be.

This is, in effect, what the error bars on graphs of poll results convey. (The precise math behind the margin of error is something we’ll talk more about in Chapter 10.) Figure 7.1 shows the results of The Pew Research Center’s final pre-election poll of 1,301 likely voters, conducted Nov. 2-5, 2000, with the reported margin of error of ±3 percentage points. The bars show the range of results that would be expected from repeated samples of the same size, assuming that the poll was conducted fairly and that the sample was representative of the population. The bars do not include uncertainty from allocating undecided voters or subsequent changes in voting intentions.

With 1,301 likely voters, the sampling uncertainty was larger than the gap between the candidates.The Pew report of the result noted that “the contest is so close that we cannot confidently predict the winner of the popular vote.” The results of the actual vote, conducted a couple of days later, was 47.9% of the popular vote went to Bush and 48.4% to Gore, both within the margin of error—but Bush won the Electoral College after the Supreme Court halted the Florida recount. As we saw in Chapter 1, real life is messier than our idealized numbers: sampling variability, people changing their minds or withholding opinions, and the workings of electoral systems all complicate what a poll can tell us about what will happen.

Sampling Error with IQ Scores

An opinion poll summarizes categorical responses with a proportion. Now we’ll turn to quantitative data. What would happen if we could repeatedly take samples from a population and summarize each sample with a quantitative statistic like the mean?

For illustration purposes, say we have a population of people with normally distributed IQ scores, with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15 (a kind of transformed distribution we mentioned in Chapter 5). We randomly collecting ten observations and find their mean. Then we draw another ten and repeat. This is effectively the same thing we did in Activity 4.3 in Chapter 4, but there we focused on bias: as we collected more samples, we tracked their individual errors—the difference between the sample mean and the true population mean—and then averaged all the errors to see how the errors for the unbiased statistics approached zero. This time we’ll collect the sample means themselves (not their errors) into a block histogram, so we can see which results occur most often and what their distribution looks like as they pile up.

We need more samples, so let’s speed things up a bit. Each tick we get a new sample of 10 individual scores, from which we compute a new sample mean. After collecting 200 samples, a pattern is becoming evident. The distribution of means piles up around the true population mean in a roughly symmetrical distribution. Most sample means are near 100, and extreme ones are rare. The spread of the distribution of means is less than that of the original population.

The name for this kind of distribution is a distribution of sample means, or sampling distribution of the mean. And the predictability of its shape is the key insight that powers the inferential statistics that researchers use.

The Distribution of Sample Means

Technically the sampling distribution of the mean is the distribution of sample means obtained by selecting all possible samples of a given sample size \(n\) from a population.

For the IQ distribution, we ended with 200 random samples, not all of the possible samples for the population. As you might imagine, for any reasonably large population, there’s often a huge number of possible samples; for practical purposes, the number of possible samples might be effectively infinite. We’ll return to this problem shortly, but we won’t let it stop us from demonstrating the fundamental principles now.

We just need a small, manageable population, from which we can obtain all possible samples of a given size. Like, very small. Suppose we have a population consisting of just four people. We’re interested in their height. We have one person 60 inches tall, one 62, one 64, and one 66.

But remember, we’re not interested in individuals anymore. We’re interested in samples. So now we’re going to list every possible sample where \(n = 2\), meaning we have two observations in each sample. All possible samples are shown in this table. \(X_1\) is the first score in the sample. \(X_2\) is the second score. And \(M\) is that sample’s mean. Note that for this to work, it is important that we’re sampling independently, meaning that the first observation in each sample does not affect the second. For this tiny population that means the same person can appear twice. The first row, for example, is the 60-inch person twice. Obviously the sample mean there is 60.

In case the tables don’t make this process entirely clear, we can visualize the process of obtaining each sample and its mean step by step.

The population is exactly four values, 60, 62, 64, and 66. If we plot them on a block histogram (well, technically a dot histogram in this case), it’s pretty simple. Just four scores for the four different heights. The frequency of each is one because there’s only one person of each height. So far, so simple.

The first ordered sample is 60 and 60. Like with the IQ samples, we compute the sample mean and add a mean block to the distribution below.

The next sample is 62 and 60, giving a sample mean of 61. The new mean block lands next to the first one down below.

The third sample is 64 and 60, for a sample mean of 62.

Now we just continue through the rest of the 16 possible samples. Each pair lands, we compute the mean, and add a block to the histogram below. After all 16 samples with replacement, the complete sampling distribution is visible.

So for this tiny population, we have been able to exhaustively compile every possible sample mean for samples of size \(n = 2\)—something we could only approximate with the bigger IQ population and the 200 means we collected randomly. But you see the similar pattern? Again, sample means close to the true population mean are relatively common; sample means far from the true population mean are relatively uncommon.

Now you might be wondering what the whole purpose of this quite tedious process was. Well, by going through the process of obtaining every possible sample mean, we now have a new kind of population: the population of sample means. And we can think about that population, and compute its characteristics, the same way we can with any other set of data.

We can, for example, figure out its mean and standard deviation. The mean should be obvious visually. It’s a symmetrical distribution and the peak is right on 63. So that’s the mean. You can also calculate it by adding all 16 sample means and dividing by 16, the number of scores. If you care to check, you’ll notice that 63 was the mean of the population as well, so the mean of the sampling distribution is equal to \(\mu\).

The mean of the distribution of sample means is called the expected value of the mean, \(\mu_M\).

We can calculate the standard deviation of the 16 means in the same way we did in Chapter 4, by putting all those values in a table, working out columns for deviations, the squared deviations, then using those to get the sum of squared deviations, the variance, and the standard deviation. Note that because we obtained every possible sample mean, this set of 16 means is itself a complete population, so we use the population formula, dividing by \(N\).

\[SS = 40, \qquad \sigma_M^2 = \frac{40}{16} = 2.5, \qquad \sigma_M = \sqrt{2.5} \approx 1.58\]

The standard deviation of the sample means is also called the standard error of the mean. The symbol for this is \(\sigma_M\).

Lastly, because we know every possible mean, we can think about the probability of observing certain sample means from this distribution, just like we thought about the probabilities of obtaining certain scores from a regular population.

The Central Limit Theorem

The trouble with distributions of sample means is that it’s just not feasible in most realistic situations to obtain every possible sample of a given size for a population. We were able to do it with a population of just four people and samples of two. But imagine computing every possible sample of a given size from a population we’d actually be interested in, like, say, American voters. Even if we had the scores of everyone in the population, which we usually don’t, the number of possible samples would be astronomical for any useful sample size. There would just be too many people, too many possible samples.

Thankfully, there is a tool that allows us to predict the characterisitcs of the distribution of sample means without having to compile every possible sample. It is called the Central Limit Theorem.

We can predict the sampling distribution’s center and variability from the population mean, population variability, and sample size. The central limit theorem adds a prediction about shape; the center and variability formulas below hold without requiring normality.

Center: The Expected Value of the Mean

Under the usual conditions—independent observations drawn in the same way from a population with a finite mean and variance—the average of all the sample means equals the population mean. This is because \(M\) is an unbiased statistic.

\[\mu_M = \mu \tag{7.1}\]

Usually we don’t draw sample after sample, and for any one sample, we won’t be exactly right every time.

Variability: The Standard Error of the Mean

The equation for the standard error of the mean is:

\[\sigma_M = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{7.2}\]

The numerator is the population standard deviation, \(\sigma\). So the larger the variability in the population, the larger the standard error of the mean. The denominator is a function of sample size (its square root). And so the larger our sample size, the smaller the standard error of the mean.

An algebraically equivalent form is useful if you have variance, \(\sigma^2\), rather than the standard deviation, \(\sigma\):

\[\sigma_M = \dfrac{\sqrt{\sigma^2}}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{7.3}\]

You can see how they are equivalent: \(\sigma^2\) is literally just \(\sigma\) squared, so \(\sqrt{\sigma^2}\) is the same as \(\sigma\).

With this equation in hand, we can just figure out the standard error of the mean using just known population characteristics. The standard deviation of the four heights is 2.24. So if we put it into that equation, we get 2.24 for the population variability divided by the square root of two, which is our sample size.

\[\sigma_M = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2.24}{\sqrt{2}} = 1.58\]

We get the same answer as when we went through the full rigmarole of compiling all possible samples for that little population and computing their standard deviation manually: 1.58! Plugging two numbers into an equation could have saved us a lot of work there.

Of course, the real value is that we can apply this equation in situations where we couldn’t compile all possible samples. Applying this to the IQ distribution that we sampled from earlier, we can see how the standard error of the mean changes as a function of sample size. Since \(n\) is in the denominator of the equation, as \(n\) increases, the standard error decreases.

Shape of the Sampling Distribution

The normal curve isn’t merely a convenient shape that a few variables happen to resemble. All kinds of quantities are influenced by many small, more or less independent contributions pushing them one way or another. There are many ways for those contributions to mostly balance, and fewer ways for them all to push in the same direction. Under suitable conditions, the result is something like the pile on the board: lots of values near the middle, progressively fewer toward the extremes, and a shape that can be modelled remarkably well by the normal distribution. This doesn’t mean that every quantity made from random contributions must be normally distributed.

Under the conditions described above, the theorem tells us how the sampling distribution’s standardized shape behaves as sample size grows.

If the population is normally distributed, its distribution of sample means is exactly normal at every sample size. If the population is not normal, averaging increasingly many independent observations usually makes the distribution of means progressively more normal. But this is convergence, not a switch: there is no sample size at which the remaining nonnormality suddenly disappears.

That is why the familiar rule of thumb \(n = 30\) needs to be handled carefully. For many moderately nonnormal populations, the remaining difference from normality is small enough by then that it has little practical consequence. For a more severely skewed or heavy-tailed population, 30 may not be enough. And even when the approximation is entirely adequate, that means negligible for the purpose at hand, not mathematically zero. The visualization below makes that distinction visible.

Add a second ruler underneath the scores, like we did in Chapter 5. Its center is \(\mu\), and each unit is one standard error, \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). The means haven’t moved. The two rulers simply describe the same positions in different units.

Let the original score ruler fade away, and move the standard-error ruler into its place. Then stretch it to fill the lower panel. The histogram follows the normal curve more closely again. On the original score scale, the means would be packed into a narrower range; here, each unit on the ruler represents the new, smaller standard error. The two tutorial population formations are fixed, precomputed representations of continuous-model densities; they are not finite rosters of people. Every simulated observation is drawn independently, and all the selectable models have finite variance. Those conditions matter. The ordinary central limit theorem does not promise convergence for every imaginable distribution or sampling process.

Sampling People in Practice

Armed with our knowledge of the sampling distribution and the Central Limit Theorem, we can predict the distribution of sample means. As we’ll see, this is the engine that powers the inferential tests we’ll begin employing in the next chapter. But it’s important to note that those predictions depend on how we collect the observations in each sample. In the demonstrations above, I always sampled with replacement. This was most obvious in the example with the small population of four heights. The 16 set of all 16 possible sample means for \(n = 2\) included samples in which the same person appeared twice.

To see why that mattered, let’s turn to another statistics textbook favorite: picking marbles out of a jar. In The Art of Conjecturing, published in 1713, Jacob Bernoulli asked his reader to imagine an urn containing 5,000 pebbles, in some unknown ratio of white to black. Urns of pebbles were used to cast votes in elections at the time, so Bernoulli may have been thinking about a very real issue, not just a quaint way of illustrating statistical ideas. By drawing pebbles repeatedly, someone who couldn’t see the contents could estimate that ratio from the proportion of white pebbles they observed (Bernoulli, 2006).

Imagine a jar with just 50 marbles, 40 blue and 10 white. If each marble is equally likely to be drawn, \(P(\text{white})=10/50=.2\).

Suppose the first marble we draw is white. What is the probability that a second draw will also be white? This raises a small but important procedural detail. Did we put the first marble back into the jar, or did we leave it out?

If we sample with replacement, we put it back and mix the jar before drawing again. There are still 10 white marbles among 50, so the probability stays .2. The draws are independent: knowing the first result doesn’t change the probabilities for the second.

If we sample without replacement, we leave the first marble out. After drawing white, only 9 white marbles and 49 marbles in total remain. The probability of another white is now \(9/49 \approx .184\). The draws are dependent, because knowing the first result changes the probabilities for the second.

A random sample is selected by a chance procedure. Random selection and independence are different ideas: both marble procedures use chance, but only replacing and mixing the marble makes successive draws independent. Our standard-error formula, \(\sigma_M=\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), assumes that each observation contributes independently to the mean. Changing the sampling procedure can change the variability of those means.

But social scientists generally aren’t sampling marbles from a jar, or dots from a simulated digital distribution. We’re interested in people: we want to see what they get up to, or ask them questions, or measure their physiological responses, or whatever. We can’t easily put a person back into the proverbial jar and give them another chance of being selected the next time, because having the same person in the sample twice would give us no new information. The same person taking the same study a second time would explicitly break the assumption of independence. So technically we’re sampling without replacement. But doesn’t that also break the assumption of independence? Not necessarily. If the sample is a small fraction of the population, the change in probabilities is negligible. For example, if we have a population of 10,000 people and we sample 100, the probability of selecting any one person changes from \(1/10,000\) to \(1/9,999\) after one draw. That difference is so small that we can treat the draws as approximately independent for practical purposes.

There are problems that just making sure our numbers are large enough can’t solve.

Other kinds of dependence need more care. Ten people from one household, or ten measurements of the same person, may share influences that make their scores similar. Treating them as ten independent observations can make a mean look more precise than it is.

Random selection also doesn’t guarantee that a study represents the population we care about. A list of phone numbers may omit some people, and those who respond may differ from those who don’t. Recruiting whoever happens to walk past, or whoever volunteers for course credit, gives us a convenience sample rather than a simple random sample of the general population. Increasing the sample size can reduce random sampling error without correcting those selection biases.

So when applying these ideas, ask how people entered the sample, what population they can reasonably represent, and whether their observations share influences. The formulas describe a sampling process; their usefulness depends on how well that process matches the study.

Summary

So to repeat and summarize, under independent sampling from a population with finite mean and variance, the distribution of sample means has an expected value, \(\mu_M\), equal to \(\mu\). It has a standard deviation, or standard error of the mean, of \(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\). Its shape is exactly normal at every \(n\) when the population is normal. For a nonnormal population, its standardized shape approaches normality gradually as \(n\) increases, at a rate that depends on the population. Thirty is often a useful convention, but it is not a magical cutoff. These insights power much of the inferential statistics that we’ll move on to later, because they allow us to reason about the probability of sample statistics rather than only individual scores.
