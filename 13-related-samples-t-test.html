13  Related Samples t Test

Two block distributions in distinct blue shades building up from paired observations.

This will be our last chapter on the \(t\) statistic. We’ll introduce the related-samples \(t\) test, used to compare two samples that, unlike those in the previous chapter, are somehow related. We’ll discuss the research designs this involves, their pros and cons, and how to calculate the test.

The competition machine

First, a little game for you to play. You’re going to turn a handle (by moving your mouse or finger in circles) to move a little blue flag along a race track. Try your best, and have fun!

Activity 13.1: The competition machine
A game based on Triplett's competition machine: wind a reel to send a flag around a track four times, first alone and then racing a rival flag. It needs JavaScript.

You’ll start by practicing moving the flag, just so you can see how the game works. In the real race you’ll do 4 laps, but for practice just 2. Click the start button when you’re ready.

Now for the real thing! You’ll do 4 laps by yourself, and your time will be recorded. Press start when you’re ready.

A challenger appears! You’ll race again, but now against the red flag! Click start when you’re ready.

How’d it go? Feel free to play again if you like, but take a moment to think about this first pair of races. Did you beat that devious red flag? Whatever your times were, did you do better when you were racing against a competitor than alone? That’s the important question for us.

This is a little digital recreation of one of my favorite studies in the history of psychology. In fact, it is often credited as being the first published experiment in social psychology—though like many such claims, that’s questionable. It was conducted by Norman Triplett as part of his master’s research at Indiana University, and published in 1898. He was interested in how racing alone or against competitors might affect performance. This is now known as social facilitation: the idea that the presence of other people can improve our performance on some kinds of tasks (Triplett, 1898).

At first, Triplett examined cycling records, comparing unpaced rides against the clock, rides with someone setting the pace, and competition races. He compared averages and graphed patterns in the data, finding some evidence that multiple cyclists racing together might produce faster times than people racing against the clock.

But Triplett realized he needed some more convincing, more carefully controlled data. So he devised the “competition machine”: a clever apparatus disguised as a children’s game. Two fishing reels were secured to the end of a Y-shaped apparatus that was clamped on top of a heavy table. Bands of silk cord were run over the axles of the reels and across two pulleys, so that turning the reel would cause a small flag sewn into the silk to move around the four-meter circuit. The task was to turn the reel and complete the circuit four times as quickly as possible. Triplett had children do this a number of times, either by themselves or facing off against another kid.

What makes the paper so charming is that Triplett included full tables of the raw data in his paper, including the first names and last initials of the children. Compared to modern results sections filled with impersonal aggregate statistics, it’s a delight to imagine little Violet F. and Willie H. staring each other down across the competition machine, teeth gritted in determination, Triplett watching on with clipboard in hand. He noted some wonderfully specific details. “An intense desire to win,” Triplett wrote, was often accompanied by “labored breathing, flushed faces and a stiffening or contraction of the muscles of the arm.” The youngest children sometimes became so excited that they could barely turn the crank; “The effort to continue turning in these cases was by a swaying of the whole body.” One particularly competitive five-year-old was so upset at losing his race, according to his mother, that he “felt badly about it all day.”

This gives us a context in which to think about research designs where each participant does something more than once, and how to analyze the resulting data.

Between- and within-participants designs

This requires some more thought about research design. In Chapter 12, we used a between-participants design: each participant experienced and contributed data to a single condition of our study. In the spending example, everyone spent their ten dollars either on themselves or on someone else, not both.

Because it’s a different group of people in each sample, there are at least two reasons why our sample means might differ. One is a treatment effect that increases or decreases scores overall. That’s what we’re hoping to discover. But one group might have a few more people who score high on our measure and the other a few more who score low, just by chance. Those individual differences are another source of variability between groups.

Now, compare that to a related-samples design, in which each score in one sample is paired with a specific score in the other. The most common version is the within-participants design, also called a repeated-measures design. Every participant experiences and contributes data to both conditions. When you played the game in Activity 13.1, you produced two scores, one alone and one in competition. That’s what Triplett got from each of the kids he tested.

There are still individual differences within each sample, but it’s the same people in both. A child who is generally slow contributes a score to both conditions, so that stable difference in overall speed doesn’t explain the difference between conditions. When the paired scores tend to go together, taking those stable differences out of the comparison can give us a more powerful test. (Other things could still change between the two measurements, though, like practice or fatigue. We’ll come back to those when we interpret the results.)

Matched-subjects designs

Another variation of the related-samples design is what’s called a matched-subjects design, or matched-samples design. We have two samples of different people, but each individual in one sample is deliberately paired with someone similar in the other.

Say we didn’t want the same kids to repeat the reel-turning task alone and in competition. We have one group of kids do it alone, and a different set of kids do it with a competitor. We might match children on age, since it’s plausible that older children could turn the reels faster. For each pair of children of the same age, we’d randomly assign one to perform alone and the other to perform in competition. Each child contributes a score in just one condition, but we compare the two scores within each matched pair. These are the pairs we use in the analysis; they aren’t the two children racing against each other.

Age is our matching variable: the characteristic we use to form the pairs. If we gave the alone and competition conditions the same mix of ages, age couldn’t explain a difference simply because one condition had older children. But children of the same age could still differ in strength, coordination, or experience. Matching approximates the advantages of measuring the same people twice, at least as far as the variables we’ve decided to match people on. It works best when those variables help predict the scores we’re measuring.

Pros and cons

There’s a practical advantage to measuring the same people twice: to get five scores in each condition, a within-participants study needs five people rather than ten. Participants can be expensive and time consuming to recruit, especially if we’re not just using intro psych students and giving them course credit. And sometimes participants are just hard to come by, like patients with a rare condition or clients at a single clinic. Getting more data out of each person can be what makes a study feasible at all. (A matched-subjects design doesn’t have that advantage; it requires twice as many participants as a within-participants design.)

A within-participants design also lets us see whether individuals change over time. Remember the cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) study for obsessive-compulsive disorder from Chapters 5 and 8? Researchers used the Dimensional Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (DOCS) to measure people’s symptoms before and after treatment (Abramowitz et al., 2010). (You’ll finally get to analyze the data from that study appropriately in the learning checks at the end of this chapter!)

But there are potential downsides to within-participants designs. People might stop treatment or miss the follow-up assessment. That’s called dropout. For the related-samples test, we need both measurements to calculate each person’s difference score, so someone with only a pretreatment score can’t contribute a complete pair. It can be necessary to recruit more people than the number of complete pairs we want to allow for this. And if those who drop out differ from those who stay—perhaps they’re the people who aren’t finding the treatment helpful—the remaining pairs could give us a misleading picture.

From two samples to one

Let’s use some of Triplett’s own data to make things concrete. His procedure was a bit more complicated than I’m making out here, but for our purposes we’ll take just the first five kids from one of the paper’s tables. Following a practice trial, all these kids first did a race alone, then with a competitor. Table 13.1 shows the data. (I’ve rounded the times to whole seconds to keep the math more manageable. Note also that the race took much longer overall on Triplett’s machine than the version you played in Activity 13.1.) Triplett grouped these five kids among those whose performance generally improved in competition, so this selected example is for learning the procedure, rather than estimating the overall effect in his study.

If we ignored the Participant column, the data would resemble the kind of scores from independent samples that we worked with in the last chapter. But the column of names tells us that each row comes from one child; the scores are related. Notice that the children differ in their overall speed. Anna P. took quite a bit longer than Willie H. in both conditions, but the comparison we’re interested in is how each child’s own time changed. We need a way to distill that information when we compare the conditions.

Let’s look at this visually, like we did for independent samples last time, before we walk through the equations.

Activity 13.2: From pairs to differences
Triplett's five children: their times alone (54, 67, 38, 46, 42 seconds) and in competition (43, 57, 39, 41, 36 seconds), then each child's difference score, competition minus alone: −11, −10, 1, −5, −6 seconds, with mean difference −6.2.

Here are the five children’s times in each condition, arranged on a horizontal axis. The condition means differ, but there is also considerable spread within each row: the SDs are 11.5 and 8.1 seconds. An independent-samples test would incorporate this into its standard error. But notice each child has the same color dot in both rows. The related-samples analysis makes use of this pairing.

Joining each child’s dots lets us focus on changes. The children differ considerably in their overall speed, but the lines show a more consistent pattern. Four of the five kids were faster in competition than alone.

To quantify this, we subtract each child’s alone time from their competition time. This centers each child’s performance around their Alone baseline, leaving just the change between that and their Competition time. Visually, that change is the horizontal distance each connecting line travels. To put all the changes on a common scale, each line slides over until its Alone end sits on zero (the dotted line, which I’ve placed beneath the average of the Alone times), then drops down and makes a new dot on the new Difference axis. The ten original times become five difference scores: −11, −10, 1, −5, and −6 seconds.

The mean of those differences is −6.2 seconds: the children were 6.2 seconds faster in competition, on average. The bracket runs from zero, the null hypothesis’s mean difference, to −6.2. This signed difference is the numerator of \(t\).

The difference scores have a standard deviation of 4.76 seconds, smaller than either set of scores on its own. (We saw those condition SDs in step 1; they’re shown again here faded.) Subtracting within each pair removes stable differences in the children’s overall speed. That doesn’t mean the differences will always have a smaller SD than either set of original scores. But when the two measurements are positively related, as they are here, using the pairs gives us a smaller standard error than treating the scores as independent.

Now we can treat the five difference scores as one single sample. As in Chapter 11, the SD gets divided by \(\sqrt{n}\) to create the standard error. The red segment shows one estimated standard error, starting at zero and extending toward the mean difference. This provides the denominator of the \(t\) statistic.

Dividing numerator by denominator relabels the lower axis in standard errors from zero. In this case, the observed mean difference represents \(\approx -2.91\) standard errors. The sign is negative because we calculated competition minus alone, and competition times were lower on average.

Treating these paired scores as independent would violate the assumption of independence. Instead, we turn them into a single sample of difference scores. Subtracting each child’s time alone from their time in competition gives the difference score, \(D\), shown in a new column:

Negative differences mean faster times in competition; positive differences mean slower times. We could subtract in the other direction, but we need to keep the direction the same for every row. Reversing it would reverse the signs of the mean difference and \(t\), without changing our two-tailed test decision.

Figure 13.4 shows this logic, using the same kind of diagram as Figure 11.3 and Figure 12.1. On the left, the design looks like the independent-samples design, with two treated samples. But now they come from a single sample: the same people receive both treatments. Subtracting within each pair turns the two treated samples into one sample of difference scores. From there, the right side is the single-sample \(t\) test again, with a subscript \(D\) added. The treated population is now a population of difference scores, and we compare its unknown mean, \(\mu_D\), with the reference value of \(0\) that the null hypothesis specifies.

The related-samples \(t\) statistic

Remember that every \(t\) statistic has the same basic structure (Equation 12.1):

\[ t = \frac{\text{sample statistic} - \text{population parameter}}{\text{estimated standard error}} \]

For the single-sample \(t\) test, the null gave us a reference value for the population mean, \(\mu_0\) (Equation 11.2):

\[ t = \frac{M - \mu_0}{s_M} \]

For the independent-samples \(t\) test, the statistic was the difference between sample means, and the null said the difference between population means was zero (Equation 12.2):

\[ t = \frac{(M_1 - M_2) - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{s_{M_1 - M_2}} \]

Conceptually, the related-samples \(t\) test is just the single-sample \(t\) test, applied to difference scores. We just add a \(D\) subscript to things to show that we’re dealing with difference scores rather than the original scores: \(M\) becomes \(M_D\), the mean of the difference scores; \(s\) becomes \(s_D\); and \(s_M\) becomes \(s_{M_D}\). The parameter becomes \(\mu_D\). As in Chapter 12, we’re writing the parameter itself because it’s about a hypothesized difference, and the null hypothesis says its value is zero:

\[ t = \frac{M_D - \mu_D}{s_{M_D}} \tag{13.1}\]

The denominator is the estimated standard error of the mean difference score, \(s_{M_D}\). Don’t confuse it with Chapter 12’s estimated standard error of the mean difference, \(s_{M_1 - M_2}\). The mean of the differences will be equal to the difference between the two condition means. For our children, the mean of the differences, \(-6.2\), is the same as the difference between the two condition means, \(43.2 - 49.4\). But the two standard errors are calculated differently, and as we’ll see later in the chapter, that can make a big difference to the resulting \(t\). As in Chapter 11, \(s_{M_D}\) estimates how much the mean of the difference scores would vary from sample to sample. To calculate it, we divide the sample standard deviation of the difference scores, \(s_D\), by the square root of the number of pairs, \(n\):

\[ s_{M_D} = \frac{s_D}{\sqrt{n}} \tag{13.2}\]

With a null mean difference of zero, we just divide \(M_D\) by its estimated standard error to see how many standard errors our observed mean difference falls from zero.

Degrees of freedom

We’ll need to know the degrees of freedom for the \(t\) test. Here it’s \(df = n - 1\), where \(n\) counts complete pairs, or difference scores. In a within-participants study, each person contributes one difference score. In a matched-subjects study, each matched pair of people contributes one difference. So our five children supply ten original times, but only five difference scores, giving \(df = 4\).

A related-samples \(t\) test, step by step

Let’s run through the familiar hypothesis testing steps with Triplett’s five kid racers, using their five difference scores as our sample.

Step 1: State the hypotheses

The null hypothesis says that the population mean difference is zero. We’ll use a nondirectional alternative, looking for a difference in either direction:

\[ H_0: \mu_D = 0 \]

\[ H_1: \mu_D \ne 0 \]

Step 2: Set the decision criteria

Our nondirectional alternative calls for a two-tailed test. The five difference scores give \(df = n - 1 = 4\) (not nine; we count pairs, not individual times). Consulting the \(t\) table, the critical values for a two-tailed \(\alpha = .05\) with four degrees of freedom are \(\pm 2.776\).

Step 3: Collect and analyze the data

To calculate our statistic, we work with the difference scores just like any single sample of scores: find their mean, then their deviations from that mean, squared deviations, sum of squares, variance, and standard deviation.

\[s_{M_D} = \frac{s_D}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{4.76}{\sqrt{5}} \approx 2.13 \]

\[ t = \frac{M_D - \mu_D}{s_{M_D}} = \frac{-6.2 - 0}{2.13} \approx -2.91 \]

Step 4: Make a decision

Our \(t = -2.91\) falls below the lower critical value of \(-2.776\), so we reject the null hypothesis. These children were faster in competition, on average.

The corresponding two-tailed \(p\)-value is approximately \(.044\), just below our \(\alpha\) of \(.05\).

Step 5: Estimate the effect, interval, and power

Effect size

As before, we should also estimate the effect size, whether or not the result is significant. Cohen’s \(d\)1 works like the single-sample version: divide the mean difference by the standard deviation of the differences:

\[ d = \frac{M_D}{s_D} = \frac{-6.2}{4.764} \approx -1.30 \]

The mean difference is about 1.3 standard deviations of the differences below zero.

Confidence interval

The confidence interval works just like the single-sample interval, too, but now we’re estimating the population mean difference:

\[ M_D \pm t \times s_{M_D} = -6.2 \pm 2.776(2.131) \approx [-12.12,\ -0.28] \]

The interval ranges from about twelve seconds faster in competition to a fraction of a second faster. That’s still a wide range. Notice that it barely excludes zero, agreeing with our two-tailed test’s \(p\)-value of about \(.044\), which only just squeaked past the \(\alpha = .05\) threshold.

Statistical power

As in Chapters 11 and 12, power is more useful for planning a future study than for describing the result we’ve already found. Say we consider a three-second difference worth detecting, in either direction. How many children would we need?

The calculation is the same as for the single-sample test in Chapter 11. But the standard deviation we need is the standard deviation of the differences, not the standard deviation of the times in either condition. Our five differences give an estimate of \(s_D = 4.76\) seconds. A three-second change is about \(3/4.76 = 0.63\) standard deviations of the differences. Using the normal approximation from Chapter 9, with a two-tailed \(\alpha = .05\) and power of .80:

\[ n \approx \frac{(1.96 + 0.84)^2}{(3/4.76)^2} \approx 19.7 \]

Rounding up, that’s 20 children, each measured in both conditions. An exact calculation using the \(t\) distribution gives 22. Here \(n\) counts pairs, so a matched-subjects study would need 22 pairs, or 44 people.

The hard part is estimating how variable the changes will be. Two conditions could each have a lot of variability, but if the same people score high in both, their differences could be much less variable. That’s what happened here. And our estimate comes from only five children, so we shouldn’t treat it as a precise description of the population.

Reporting and interpreting the results

Reporting results

As usual, we need to report the descriptive statistics, the kind of test, the estimated difference and its confidence interval, the test statistic with its degrees of freedom, the \(p\)-value or whether it falls below \(\alpha\), and the effect size. Here’s how we might report our worked example:

In this selected five-child example, children completed the task faster in competition (\(M = 43.2\), \(SD = 8.14\) seconds) than alone (\(M = 49.4\), \(SD = 11.48\) seconds). A two-tailed related-samples \(t\) test found a significant mean difference, \(M_D = -6.20\) seconds, 95% CI [-12.12, -0.28], \(t(4) = -2.91\), \(p < .05\), \(d = -1.30\).

Assumptions of the related-samples \(t\) test

We’ve encountered the assumption of independence quite a few times by now. This applies to the related-samples \(t\) test as well, but in a slightly different way. Obviously the whole point is that the samples are not independent of one another; they consist of pairs of scores from the same person (or from participants we have matched on some characteristic). We expect the two scores within a pair to be related, not independent; that’s why we put them together. But that’s fine, because we don’t use those samples themselves for the analysis. We use the difference scores instead. The independence assumption applies to those: the difference scores must be independent of one another. One child’s change shouldn’t depend on another child’s change.

There’s also the familiar assumption about normality. The \(t\) distribution assumes a normally distributed population, but here it’s the distribution of difference scores that matters, not the distribution of the individual scores in either condition. As usual, there is some leeway for non-normality, particularly with larger samples, but strong skew, bimodality, or outliers can still be problematic, so the data should be checked carefully.

Interpreting the results

The result was “statistically significant,” in a direction consistent with social facilitation: better race performance against a competitor. But as usual, we would still need to be cautious in our interpretation. A statistically significant result doesn’t rule out a Type I error, and the wide confidence interval shows how imprecise our estimate is with just five children. We ought also to be careful about generalizing the result to other children. For the data we analyzed, I specifically selected five kids Triplett classified as “stimulated positively” by competition. That selection prevents trustworthy inferences about a more general population of children.

There’s also a problem with the particular pairs of scores we analyzed: every child did the alone trial first and the competition trial second. Maybe some of the improvement was just getting better at turning the reel through practice. Or maybe they were getting tired, which could push their times in the other direction. This is an order effect: a change in performance or responses caused by the order in which the conditions are experienced. (Triplett anticipated this problem. Each child completed six timed trials, and he varied the sequence of alone and competition trials across two groups. But everyone started alone (Triplett, 1898). Our first-pair-only analysis leaves out his attempts to separate practice from competition.)

One way to deal with order effects is counterbalancing. In a simple two-condition study, we could have some children do the alone trial first and others do competition first. That way, extra practice or fatigue on the second run wouldn’t always go along with the same condition. Using the same children twice removes stable differences in their overall speed, but it doesn’t remove things that change between runs.

But some experiences have lasting effects that counterbalancing can’t undo. Think about the CBT study: we can’t have someone do therapy first and then go back to being the same untreated person they were before. For some questions, a between-participants design makes more sense.

So our significant result here doesn’t establish an overall effect in Triplett’s data. But what’s the status of the research? Triplett didn’t even have tools like the \(t\) test to analyze data. A later reanalysis of his published data using modern statistical approaches found little convincing evidence that competition improved performance overall (Strube, 2005).

Triplett gave us an ingenious way to investigate an interesting question, even if his results didn’t settle it. But the competition machine got another outing. In 2025, researchers reported a replication with 445 children using an apparatus built from Triplett’s diagrams. Children were faster alongside another child than alone, even after accounting for practice, though they weren’t explicitly told to compete (McHugh et al., 2025). It’s still not conclusive—no single piece of research is. But it’s encouraging evidence for the idea Triplett was pursuing.

What’s the difference?

To finish up, let’s briefly forget everything we just learned about related samples, and subject the same data we just worked with to an independent-samples \(t\) test, as if we hadn’t realized that each row belonged to one child. Let’s see what happens using Chapter 12’s equations, treating the two sets of times as independent samples.

With a two-tailed test, \(\alpha = .05\), and \(df = 5 + 5 - 2 = 8\), our critical \(t\) values would be \(\pm 2.306\). The competition times constitute sample 1, with \(M_1 = 43.2\) and variance \(s_1^2 = 66.2\); the alone times are sample 2, with \(M_2 = 49.4\) and variance \(s_2^2 = 131.8\). The pooled variance weights each variance by its degrees of freedom:

\[ s_p^2 = \frac{df_1 s_1^2 + df_2 s_2^2}{df_1 + df_2} = \frac{4(66.2) + 4(131.8)}{4 + 4} = 99 \]

The estimated standard error of the mean difference is \(s_{M_1 - M_2} = \sqrt{99/5 + 99/5} \approx 6.29\). And the \(t\) statistic works out to \(-6.2/6.29 \approx -0.99\), so this calculation would fail to reject the null hypothesis.

Here’s how that compares with the correct analysis using the pairs we did earlier:

The mean of the differences is exactly the difference between the sample means, so both numerators are the same. What changes is the variability we used to calculate the denominator. The independent-samples calculation includes how much the children differ in their overall speed. The related-samples calculation uses how much their changes differ, excluding stable individual differences in overall speed. Here that’s a much smaller number, so we get a smaller standard error and a narrower confidence interval, even though the lower degrees of freedom require a more extreme critical \(t\).

Obviously which variety of \(t\) test we use isn’t a choice we make by trying both out and keeping the answer we prefer. My point is that the numbers don’t know or care what we subject them to. We have to know where those numbers came from, and what the appropriate statistical tools are. These scores belong in pairs, so our analysis needs to use the differences. The related-samples approach doesn’t guarantee greater power, but when related scores do tend to go together, the analysis is not only appropriate, but also more powerful, as is the case for our data here.

Suppose that in a study like Triplett’s, children’s times alone and in competition were unrelated: knowing how fast a child was alone told you nothing about how fast they’d be in competition. Compared with treating the two conditions as independent samples, what would analyzing the pairs do to the estimated standard error?

Response options

Make it much smaller

Leave it about the same

Make it much larger

Hint

What does subtracting within each pair take out of the comparison? If a child’s time alone tells you nothing about their time in competition, is there anything to take out?

Answer

Leave it about the same

Explanation

Pairing shrinks the standard error by taking stable differences between people out of the comparison. If the two scores don’t go together, there’s nothing to take out: the difference scores vary about as much as the independent-samples calculation assumes. And with half the degrees of freedom, the critical value would be more extreme, so pairing wouldn’t help here.

6

For the CBT calculation, would you reject the null hypothesis in a two-tailed test at \(\alpha = .05\)? The critical values for \(df = 39\) are \(\pm 2.023\). What does the result tell us about symptoms, and does it establish that therapy caused the change?

Answer

With 40 complete pairs, \(df = 39\). Our \(t \approx -9.57\) falls below \(-2.023\), so we reject the null hypothesis. The mean symptom score was significantly lower after treatment. But everyone was measured before treatment and then after, and that order can’t be counterbalanced: people can’t go back to being untreated. Symptoms might have improved over time anyway, or people might answer the questionnaire differently the second time. To show that the therapy caused the change, we’d want a comparison group of people who weren’t treated.

7

We’re testing a null population mean difference of zero, and the estimated standard error is greater than zero. What would a related-samples \(t\) statistic of 0 tell us? Would it mean nobody’s score changed?

Answer

The sample mean difference is exactly zero. Individual difference scores can still be positive or negative and cancel each other out. It doesn’t mean every person had identical scores in both conditions, or establish that the population mean difference is zero.

8

Think of a research question you could study either by measuring the same people in two conditions or by comparing two separate groups. Briefly describe each version of the study. What would the related-samples version gain, and what problems might it introduce?

Hint

Think about how many people each version needs, the differences between people, what might change between the two measurements, and whether everyone will come back for the second one.

Example answer

Does caffeine speed up reaction times? In the independent-samples version, one group drinks coffee and another drinks decaf before a reaction time test. In the related-samples version, everyone does the test on two days, once after each drink. The related version needs half as many people, and it takes stable differences in people’s speed out of the comparison, which could make the test more powerful. But people might get faster with practice on the second day, so we’d counterbalance which drink comes first. And some people might not come back for the second session.

Explanation

A good answer recognizes that the related version helps most when people’s scores in the two conditions tend to go together, and that anything that changes between the two measurements, like practice, fatigue, or lingering effects of the first condition, needs to be dealt with. For some questions, like whether a therapy works, the first condition can’t be undone, and two separate groups make more sense.
