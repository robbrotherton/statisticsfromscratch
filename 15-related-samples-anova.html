15  Related-samples ANOVA

Three shaded normal curves in distinct blue shades grow at a shared center, then separate.

In Chapter 14 we introduced the general logic of ANOVA and used its independent-samples variant to analyze data from a between-participants design. But like the \(t\) test, ANOVA has varieties for different situations. In this chapter we’ll introduce the related-samples ANOVA. Just like the related-samples \(t\) test, this is for within-participants or matched-subjects designs. The basic logic is the same as the independent-samples ANOVA. To deal with related samples, we just extend the idea of partitioning variance to account for differences between people.

Off to the races, again

We spent Chapter 13 looking at data from a handful of kids who had completed a fun little flag race twice, first alone and then against another kid. Our \(t\) test found that they were generally a little faster racing against a rival, consistent with a social facilitation effect. But we left a loose end. Was it the competition that spurred them to race quicker, or was it just practice at turning the reel? With just two races from each kid, and competition always coming after the solo race, we couldn’t tell.

How could we tease apart any effects of practice and competition? Perhaps we could add another race. If the kids raced alone twice before competing, we could see how much they improved from the repetition alone, then see whether competition provoked any additional improvement. A bigger improvement in the third race would fit the social facilitation explanation.

In fact, Norman Triplett gave each child six timed trials, with different sequences of solo and competition trials (Triplett, 1898). Here we’ll use the first three trials from five kids who all followed the same order: alone, alone, then competition, as shown in Table 15.1.1

With two scores per kid in Chapter 13, we just subtracted one from the other to create a single difference score. Now each kid has three scores. Simple pairwise subtraction can’t handle the whole set of scores in a single test. But you know what can: ANOVA. We’ll eventually perform post-hoc tests to look at each pairwise comparison in more detail, as we did in Chapter 14, but ANOVA provides the initial omnibus test.

Let’s think about how the independent-samples ANOVA from last chapter would handle this data. It would group them by race type—the columns of Table 15.1—and compare the differences between the three column means with the variability within each column: between groups versus within groups. Eyeballing it, it looks like there’s a lot of variability within each column. Like, check out Clyde G.’s times. Clyde was not messing around—he totally demolished the other kids’ times in every one of his races. So within each column the times are spread over something like twenty seconds, while the column means are just a few seconds apart.

But what if we group the scores in another way: across the table rows instead of down the columns, turning each child’s three times into a group of their own? Clyde’s three times were 36, 35, and 32 seconds. Grouped like that, his scores hardly vary at all. What are the sources of variability when we group scores in these various ways?

Partitioning variance in related samples

The idea is that the same 15 scores vary for different reasons, depending on how we group them. Clyde’s overall speed adds variability within every condition, but it can’t explain differences between the condition means since he’s in all three. The related-samples ANOVA allows us to separate out that source of variability, without losing the differences we’re interested in.

In Chapter 14 we split the total sum of squares into between-groups and within-groups components. Any treatment effect contributes between groups; chance contributes both between and within groups. But I was glossing over “chance” there. I said it is made up of sampling error and other reasons people’s scores differ. Well, one of those reasons is consistent individual differences between kids. Figure 15.1 labels that separately from the remaining chance.

With a between-participants design and independent-samples ANOVA, we can only split the total into between-groups and within-groups variability. Individual differences will contribute to within-groups variability (because each group is made up of a bunch of different people), and to between-groups variability (because each group is a different set of participants).

A within-participants design changes this. We still have a bunch of different people within each group, so individual differences contribute to within-groups variability. Clyde G.’s super-speed makes each group substantially more variable. But now the same people appear in every condition. Whatever it is about Clyde that makes him so much faster than the other kids, he brings it with him in all three races; it is consistent across groups. So the design itself eliminates individual differences as a source of variability between groups. The only things that can make the condition means differ are the treatment effect and the remaining chance.

As a result, we have a slight problem. If we used the same denominator for the \(F\) ratio as we did with independent samples, the numerator and denominator would no longer be conceptually balanced. The numerator would represent treatment plus the remaining chance, no individual differences. The denominator would represent the remaining chance plus individual differences. But the good news is that we can mathematically extract those individual differences from the denominator as well, leaving a balanced ratio.

That’s what taking each child’s three times as a group achieves. It allows us to split the within-groups sum of squares into two components: between-people variability, \(SS_{\text{people}}\), and the remaining error, \(SS_{\text{error}}\). Error is the variability that’s left once we know which child and which race a score belongs to. Children might respond a bit differently to practice or competition, or have a particularly good or bad run. Those departures from the average pattern are what Figure 15.2 calls “remaining chance.”

This additional partition gives us a new denominator for the \(F\) ratio. We divide \(SS_{\text{error}}\) by its degrees of freedom to get \(MS_{\text{error}}\). We still use the same numerator, \(MS_{\text{between}}\), but we divide it by \(MS_{\text{error}}\) rather than the full \(MS_{\text{within}}\). Under the null hypothesis, both estimate the same remaining variance. A difference between the population means can make the numerator larger, just as before.

Calculating related-samples ANOVA

Happily, the math is relatively straightforward. But before we get to the new equations, let’s see the process in action using the data from the five kids in Table 15.1. The key is regrouping the scores. The independent samples analysis only grouped them by condition; for the between-people component in Activity 15.1 we’ll group them by participant.

Activity 15.1: Where the individual differences go
Interactive diagram of five children timed in three conditions. The scores regroup into one row per child, each child's scores slide to that child's mean, and the squared deviations fill a people layer at the bottom of the within-groups sum of squares. What remains above it is error, and only the between-groups and error layers are divided by their degrees of freedom.

Here are the five children’s times, grouped by condition. Each child contributes one score to each group. We start with the same calculations as in Chapter 14, so for now the dots are colored by race type rather than by child.

As in Chapter 14, we start with each score’s deviation from the grand mean. Squaring and adding them gives \(SS_{\text{total}}\). Note that the summed squares aren’t drawn to the same scale as the individual deviations and their squares; this is just a visual representation of the logic rather than literally summing the areas.

Measuring deviations from each condition’s own mean gives \(SS_{\text{within}}\). The spread within each group includes individual differences—the fact that some children are generally faster than others. We’ll separate that out in a moment.

The differences between condition means account for \(SS_{\text{between}}\). Because of the within-participants design, the between-groups variability does not include individual differences. That’s a product of the design, not any new math.

Now for the new part. We will use the relatedness of the scores from each kid to separate the individual-differences part of the within-groups variability. We’ll color the dots by child. See the orange dots, all the way to the left of the others? That’s Clyde, smoking the competition and adding variability within groups.

Now we regroup the scores, taking each child’s three times as a group of its own, to make the relatedness more visually obvious. We’re about to do much the same as we did for between-groups variability, but comparing the children’s means instead of condition means.

Now we find each kid’s average across their three scores. If individual differences were the only source of variability—no differences between groups and no other chance error at all—then each kid’s time would be exactly the same in every race. So we imagine that all three of their scores are equal to their own average time.

Now we find the deviation of each child’s mean from the grand mean. Is this looking familiar?

Next we square each deviation. Larger squares represent children whose overall times were farther from the grand mean, whether faster or slower.

And finally we add those squares, counting each one three times to represent the child’s three scores. They add up to \(SS_{\text{people}}\), part of the within-groups variability we already counted. (It has to be in there: the between-groups component doesn’t include individual differences.) Now we’re basically done. There’s a little sliver of unaccounted variability left over. That’s the error component.

To quantify the leftover variability, we shift each kid’s cluster of scores so that each child’s mean aligns with the grand mean. Each child becomes exactly average, without those pesky individual differences pushing them apart. Notice that Clyde doesn’t look exceptional any more. But each kid’s three scores still vary from one another. That variation includes the differences between conditions. To get rid of that as well and leave just the error, we’ll look at how far each adjusted score falls from its condition’s mean.

So now we arrange the adjusted scores back into their original condition groups. The condition means are the same as they were before, but the spread around them is much smaller, because the children’s adjusted times no longer differ in overall speed. We removed those individual differences, but left the remaining chance variability and any treatment effect.

Now we find each adjusted score’s deviation from its condition mean and square it. This removes the treatment effect, so all that’s left is error.

The squares fill the remaining part of \(SS_{\text{within}}\), showing \(SS_{\text{error}}\) itself. We could have obtained the same answer by just subtracting \(SS_{\text{people}}\) from \(SS_{\text{within}}\). Or, put another way, \(SS_{\text{people}} + SS_{\text{error}} = SS_{\text{within}}\).

Let’s put it all together. \(SS_{\text{total}}\) now has three parts: between groups, between people, and error.

For the \(F\) ratio, we take the between-groups and error components. We leave individual differences behind and use only the error component of the within-groups variability as our denominator.

As usual, we divide each sum by its degrees of freedom to get the mean squares. In this case \(df_{\text{between}} = 2\) and \(df_{\text{error}} = 8\); we’ll see where that 8 comes from shortly. Our numerator mean square is much bigger than the new denominator mean square, so our \(F\) ratio will be large.

Sums of squares and degrees of freedom

So here’s how we calculate this modified \(F\) ratio for related-samples data.2

As Activity 15.1 showed, the equations and degrees of freedom for \(SS_{\text{total}}\), \(SS_{\text{between}}\), and \(SS_{\text{within}}\) are the same as in Chapter 14.

\[ \begin{align} SS_{\text{total}} &= \Sigma (X - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ SS_{\text{within}} &= \Sigma (X - M_{\text{group}})^2 \\ SS_{\text{between}} &= \Sigma n_{\text{group}}(M_{\text{group}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ \\ df_{\text{total}} &= N - 1 \\ df_{\text{within}} &= N - k \\ df_{\text{between}} &= k - 1 \end{align} \]

The only difference is that we will now split the within-groups variability into two components: between-people and error. The equation3 for between people has the same form as \(SS_{\text{between}}\), with person means in place of group means:

\[ SS_{\text{people}} = \Sigma k(M_{\text{person}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \]

\(M_{\text{person}}\) is the mean of one person’s scores across the conditions. We square each person’s deviation from the grand mean and multiply by \(k\), the number of conditions, since each person’s mean represents \(k\) scores. Then we add them up. Same idea as between groups, just regrouping the scores by person.

The final component, error, is what’s left after taking individual differences out of within-groups variability. It will provide our denominator, and it’s easiest to find by subtraction:

\[ SS_{\text{error}} = SS_{\text{within}} - SS_{\text{people}} \]

Or, as Activity 15.1 showed, we can get the same answer directly, from each score’s deviation from its group mean once that person’s overall deviation from the grand mean has been taken out:

\[ SS_{\text{error}} = \Sigma (X - M_{\text{person}} - M_{\text{group}} + M_{\text{grand}})^2 \]

For each score, subtract that child’s mean and add the grand mean: \(X - M_{\text{person}} + M_{\text{grand}}\). Stephen’s mean is 1.33 seconds above the grand mean, so we subtract 1.33 from each of his times. His three scores move by the same amount, and their differences stay the same. Doing that for each participant removes the consistent differences between them. Then we subtract the group mean from each adjusted score, square, and add them up to get the error sum of squares.

The two new components have degrees of freedom of their own. For \(SS_{\text{people}}\), we computed the deviation of each person’s mean from the grand mean. Here \(n\) is the number of people,4 so that’s \(n\) means, deviating from a single grand mean:

\[ df_{\text{people}} = n - 1 \]

Degrees of freedom are partitioned just like sums of squares, so we can find \(df_{\text{error}}\) by subtraction too:

\[ \begin{aligned} df_{\text{error}} &= df_{\text{within}} - df_{\text{people}} \\ &= (N - k) - (n - 1) \end{aligned} \]

Why subtract \(n - 1\), rather than one for every person? The condition means have already used up \(k\) degrees of freedom, leaving \(N - k\) within groups. Now we’re accounting for how far each person’s mean is from the grand mean. Those deviations must add up to zero, so once we know \(n - 1\) of them, the last is fixed. Accounting for the people component therefore uses another \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom. For our five children and three conditions, that leaves \((15 - 3) - (5 - 1) = 8\) for error.

Since each of the \(n\) people contributes \(k\) scores, \(N = nk\). Substituting that into the equation gives a shorter way to write the same thing:

\[ \begin{aligned} df_{\text{error}} &= (nk - k) - (n - 1) \\ &= (k - 1)(n - 1) \end{aligned} \]

Mean squares and \(F\)

Once we have the sums of squares and degrees of freedom, the rest is easy. We divide each relevant sum of squares by its degrees of freedom to get the mean squares, then take their ratio to get \(F\). The numerator is the same as before; the denominator is now \(MS_{\text{error}}\), rather than \(MS_{\text{within}}\).

\[ \begin{align} MS_{\text{between}} &= \frac{SS_{\text{between}}}{df_{\text{between}}} \\ MS_{\text{error}} &= \frac{SS_{\text{error}}}{df_{\text{error}}} \end{align} \]

\[ F = \frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{error}}} \]

Interpreting the \(F\) ratio is also the same as with independent samples. A large numerator relative to the denominator is evidence against the null hypothesis.

By the way, with just two conditions, computing both the related-samples \(t\) and related-samples ANOVA gives \(F = t^2\). Just as in Chapter 14, we’re expressing the same evidence in a different way.

A related-samples ANOVA, step by step

Let’s run through a full related-samples hypothesis test using ANOVA for the five children in Table 15.1. This kind of analysis is often called a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA. (One-way meaning we just have a single independent variable, repeated-measures meaning the same participants are measured in each condition.)

Step 1: State the hypotheses

As always, we start by stating our hypotheses. The null here is that the population mean time is the same in all three conditions:

\[ H_0: \mu_{\text{first alone}} = \mu_{\text{second alone}} = \mu_{\text{competition}} \]

The alternative hypothesis is that at least one condition differs from at least one other.

Step 2: Set the decision criteria

Step two is to determine the critical value for the test, with \(\alpha = .05\). We use the same \(F\) table as in Chapter 14, with the degrees of freedom for our numerator and new denominator:

\[ df_{\text{between}} = k - 1 = 2 \]

\[ df_{\text{error}} = (k - 1)(n - 1) = 2(4) = 8 \]

In Table 15.2, the column for 2 and the row for 8 give a critical \(F\) of 4.46.

Note that if these 15 scores had come from independent samples, the denominator would have had 12 degrees of freedom and the critical value would have been 3.89. Our lower degrees of freedom mean we need a more extreme \(F\) to reject the null, just as we saw with the related-samples \(t\) test.

Step 3: Collect and analyze the data

Calculating \(F\) by hand is a little labor-intensive, but a summary table helps, as in Chapter 14, with additional rows for between-people and error variability. Table 15.3 collects the equations for each element.

To save you some trouble, Table 15.4 shows the five children’s times again, with each condition’s mean and sum of squares.

And here are \(n\), \(k\), \(N\), and \(M_{\text{grand}}\) too. Remember that we have five children but 15 scores.

\[ \begin{aligned} N &= 15 \\ n &= 5 \\ k &= 3 \\ M_{\text{grand}} &= 46.67 \end{aligned} \]

(I’ve rounded the values I’m showing to two decimal places, but the calculations use the unrounded values, so you’ll need to do the same if you want your answers to match mine exactly.)

Now for the sums of squares and their degrees of freedom. We’ll start out the same as with independent samples, calculating the total, between groups, and within groups components.

Squaring each score’s deviation from the grand mean and adding them gives the total sum of squares:

\[ SS_{\text{total}} = \Sigma (X - M_{\text{grand}})^2 = 783.33 \]

\[ df_{\text{total}} = N - 1 = 15 - 1 = 14 \]

For within groups, we can just add the three condition sums of squares from Table 15.4:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{within}} &= \Sigma (X - M_{\text{group}})^2 \\ &= 272.80 + 201.20 + 153.20 \\ &= 627.20 \end{aligned} \]

\[ df_{\text{within}} = N - k = 15 - 3 = 12 \]

For between groups, we find each condition mean’s deviation from the grand mean, square it, and multiply by the five scores it represents:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{between}} &= \Sigma n_{\text{group}}(M_{\text{group}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ &\approx 5(3.53)^2 + 5(0.73)^2 \\ &\quad {}+ 5(-4.27)^2 \\ &\approx 156.13 \end{aligned} \]

\[ df_{\text{between}} = k - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2 \]

Now for the new component, \(SS_{\text{people}}\). Table 15.5, a little further down, lists each child’s mean under their name, and the People column shows the squared deviation of that mean from the grand mean. Each kid’s three rows get the same value, which is why multiplying the one squared deviation by \(k\) works. For Stephen, for example:

\[ \begin{gathered} k(M_{\text{person}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ = 3(48.00 - 46.67)^2 \approx 5.33 \end{gathered} \]

Doing the same for each of the other children and adding the five contributions gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{people}} &= \Sigma k(M_{\text{person}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ &\approx 5.33 + 75.00 + 56.33 \\ &\quad {}+ 456.33 + 8.33 \\ &\approx 601.33 \end{aligned} \]

\[ df_{\text{people}} = n - 1 = 5 - 1 = 4 \]

Then we can find \(SS_{\text{error}}\) by subtracting \(SS_{\text{people}}\) from \(SS_{\text{within}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{error}} &= SS_{\text{within}} - SS_{\text{people}} \\ &\approx 627.20 - 601.33 \\ &\approx 25.87 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} df_{\text{error}} &= df_{\text{within}} - df_{\text{people}} \\ &= 12 - 4 = 8 \end{aligned} \]

But let’s also see how the error component builds up from the individual scores, starting with one of Stephen’s. Remember Stephen’s adjusted first-alone time was 49.67 seconds. The first-alone mean stayed at 50.20. So his adjusted score is \(49.67 - 50.20 = -0.53\) seconds from that condition’s mean. Squaring that deviation gives about 0.28. Table 15.5 shows both the people squares and these error squares for every score.

With all the sums of squares and degrees of freedom in hand, we can get the two mean squares:

\[ \begin{align} MS_{\text{between}} &= \frac{SS_{\text{between}}}{df_{\text{between}}} = \frac{156.13}{2} = 78.07 \\ MS_{\text{error}} &= \frac{SS_{\text{error}}}{df_{\text{error}}} = \frac{25.87}{8} = 3.23 \end{align} \]

Finally, we divide the between-groups mean square by the error mean square:

\[ F = \frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{error}}} = \frac{78.07}{3.23} \approx 24.14 \]

Step 4: Make a decision

So now we compare our \(F\) ratio to the critical value. Our \(F\) of \(24.14\) is well beyond \(4.46\), so we reject the null hypothesis. There is a significant difference among our three conditions. The corresponding \(p\)-value is about \(.0004\), so \(p\) is less than \(\alpha\).

Step 5: Estimate the effect, compare the conditions, and consider power

Effect size: partial \(\eta^2\)

In Chapter 14 we used \(\eta^2\), the between-groups sum of squares as a proportion of the total. If you picture all the variability in the data as one big bucket, full to the brim, \(\eta^2\) gave the proportion filled by between-groups variability.

For related-samples ANOVA, we use partial eta squared, \(\eta^2_p\). We take our full bucket and dump out \(SS_{\text{people}}\) first, leaving only the between-groups and error components. Then we ask what proportion of the new total is between groups.

More mathematically, the remaining total is \(SS_{\text{between}} + SS_{\text{error}}\). That’s the same as \(SS_{\text{total}} - SS_{\text{people}}\). So:

\[\eta^2_p = \frac{SS_{\text{between}}}{SS_{\text{between}} + SS_{\text{error}}} = \frac{156.13}{156.13 + 25.87} = .86\]

So about 86% of that remaining variability, after accounting for individual differences, is attributable to the difference between the race conditions.

If we had used the ordinary \(\eta^2\), it would have come to \(.20\). The partial \(\eta^2_p\) gives a much bigger answer, because its denominator was so much smaller with \(SS_{\text{people}}\) removed. How different the answers will be depends on how much of the variability in the data comes from individual differences. If our five kids had all been about as quick as one another, \(SS_{\text{people}}\) would have been much smaller, and ordinary \(\eta^2\) much bigger, even with exactly the same average difference between conditions.5

Post-hoc tests

As you know, the ANOVA is an omnibus test: it tells us the population means aren’t all equal, but not which ones differ. You’ll also recall that Tukey’s HSD is one kind of post-hoc test. It gives us the minimum difference between means that counts as significant, while controlling the chance of at least one Type I error across all the pairwise comparisons. We can use the same approach here, adjusted for the related-samples design.

In Chapter 14, we used \(MS_{\text{within}}\) as part of computing Tukey’s HSD, since it was the representation of chance variability used in the denominator of the \(F\) ratio. Here we use \(MS_{\text{error}}\), because we’ve set aside differences in the children’s overall speed. It’s the same adjustment we made for the \(F\) ratio. We also use the error degrees of freedom when looking up the critical \(q\).

In Table 15.7, find the column for three conditions and the row for \(df_{\text{error}} = 8\). Their intersection gives the critical studentized range value, \(q = 4.04\), for \(\alpha = .05\).

And \(n = 5\), the number of scores in each condition:

\[HSD = q \sqrt{\frac{MS_{\text{error}}}{n}} = 4.04 \sqrt{\frac{3.23}{5}} \approx 3.25\]

So a difference between means greater than 3.25 seconds is significant at the \(.05\) level. For our data:

Comparison Absolute difference Exceeds 3.25 seconds?
First alone vs. second alone 2.80 seconds No
Second alone vs. competition 5.00 seconds Yes
First alone vs. competition 7.80 seconds Yes

The two gaps involving competition exceed the HSD, but the gap between the solo trials doesn’t. The children improved by an average of 2.80 seconds before competing, and by another 5.00 seconds when competition began.

Statistical power

As before, statistical power is the probability of detecting a difference among the population means when there really is one. Accounting for individual differences can help with the test’s power; when they play a big role, as they did in our data, removing them makes for a more powerful test. But a related-samples design doesn’t automatically give us more power. The advantage depends on how much the scores go together across conditions.

Think about Clyde again. He was faster than the other kids in every race, so separating out differences in overall speed removes a lot of that within-groups variability. That can give us a smaller denominator than we’d get by treating the same scores as independent. (Analyzed as independent samples, these 15 scores give just \(F(2, 12) = 1.49\), nowhere near the critical 3.89.) But separating out the people component also costs degrees of freedom, which raises the critical \(F\). The reduction in error needs to be enough to compensate. If people’s scores in the different conditions weren’t related, we’d generally end up with a similar error mean square to the independent-samples analysis, but fewer degrees of freedom and less power. As in Chapter 13, the design tells us which analysis to use; we don’t try both and keep whichever gives the result we prefer.

Statistical software can calculate power for a related-samples ANOVA, or work backwards to estimate how many participants we’d need for a target such as 80% power. I don’t want to get into the math, but generally speaking, we specify differences worth detecting, estimate the variability and how strongly scores go together across conditions, and choose an alpha level. Then software runs the numbers and tells us how many participants to recruit to achieve the desired power.

Reporting and interpreting results

So finally, we’re ready to report the results. As usual, the report needs descriptives for each condition; a statement of what test was performed and whether it was significant; the test statistic itself with degrees of freedom; \(p\) compared to \(\alpha\); and effect size. And here we summarize which post-hoc comparisons were significant and which weren’t.

A single-factor, related-samples ANOVA revealed a significant difference among children’s times on their first race alone (\(M = 50.20\), \(SD = 8.26\) seconds), a second race alone (\(M = 47.40\), \(SD = 7.09\) seconds), and a race in competition (\(M = 42.40\), \(SD = 6.19\) seconds); \(F(2, 8) = 24.14\), \(p < .001\), \(\eta^2_p = .86\). Tukey HSD comparisons indicated that children were significantly faster in competition than on either solo trial. The two solo trials did not differ significantly.

What kind of graph would work best to visualize results like these? A simple bar graph showing condition means with confidence intervals wouldn’t convey the relatedness of the scores. Really we’re interested in how consistently each child’s time changed across the conditions, rather than treating the conditions as independent.

Figure 15.4 emphasizes that. Each colored line follows one child across the races. Every line falls at both steps, even though the children start at quite different levels. Lower times mean faster performances. But the graph leaves out confidence intervals, so the statistical results still need to accompany it.

Interpreting the results

How might we interpret the results of our ANOVA and post-hoc tests? At the start of the chapter, I framed the second solo race as a way of teasing apart practice and competition. Do the findings support the idea that competition leads to greater improvement than practice alone? Well, our analysis is suggestive. A nonsignificant solo-to-solo comparison doesn’t mean practice had no effect. Maybe it was just too small to reliably detect with five children. But the significant improvement in the competition race compared to either solo race fits the idea that a rival helped the children speed up.

Being critical, though, we can pick holes in the design. A significant change after a nonsignificant one doesn’t establish that the two changes differ from one another. The question I posed was whether the competition race brought more improvement than practice alone would. That’s a comparison between the two changes—the 2.80-second drop and the 5.00-second drop—and neither the ANOVA nor Tukey’s test looks at the difference in differences. (There are ways of testing that comparison directly, though it’s beyond what we’re covering here. With these five kids it wouldn’t come close to significance.)

There’s also the order problem. Competition always came third for these kids, so we don’t know how fast they would have gone on a third trial alone. The repeated practice of the solo races could explain some or all of the improvement we attributed to competition.

So what would a better study look like? First, we’d want to vary the order properly. Triplett went partway there. His two groups of children followed different sequences, but both started with a solo race, so competition never came first. Ideally some kids would compete first, some second, and some third, so that practice was spread evenly across the conditions rather than piling up in one of them.

But that brings a problem of its own: a competition race might itself affect a later solo race. Triplett noted that the faster performance some children showed in competition endured into their later alone races, as if the rival unlocked some hidden potential that didn’t go away when the competition was over (Triplett, 1898). So we might also want more solo races, to map out how much kids improve with practice alone, or a separate group of kids who never compete at all, for comparison. And of course we’d want a lot more than five kids (who I deliberately cherry-picked to make a nice teaching example). Every design decision has strengths and weaknesses, and part of interpreting results is being clear about which explanations are supported or ruled out by the design.

Assumptions

The same sort of considerations apply to ANOVA with related samples as in Chapter 14 where we used ANOVA with independent samples, with some slight tweaks to accommodate the relatedness of the scores.

First, there’s the assumption of independence. This now applies only between people, not between conditions. We expect scores from the same child to be related across the different conditions, but different children’s responses should be independent of one another.

There is an assumption of normality, but it applies specifically to the error variability—the deviations we saw in the Error steps of Activity 15.1. Those were the deviations left over once differences between people and between conditions were set aside. We don’t need scores within each condition to be normally distributed because the analysis removes the between-people variability. Clyde’s super-speed skewed the distributions of scores within each condition, but the adjusted-score error deviations were more normal-looking.

Lastly, remember Chapter 14’s homogeneity of variance assumption: we needed comparable variability across groups. Here we have a similar requirement, but it applies to the difference scores for every pair of conditions, rather than to the scores within each condition. The requirement is called sphericity: the population variances of those difference scores should be the same. (This wasn’t an issue with the related-samples \(t\) test because with only two conditions, there’s just one set of difference scores, so no different variabilities to compare.)

Imagine that kids generally improved by a few seconds from their first solo race to their second, some kids a bit more, some a bit less, but generally in that ballpark. But suppose competition helped some children a lot and hindered others. The alone-to-competition difference scores would be much more variable than the alone-alone differences. A single pooled estimate of all that variability wouldn’t represent both kinds of change equally well. The average changes can differ—a bigger improvement from the second solo race to competition than from the first solo race to the second, for example. It’s the spread around those averages that needs to be about the same from one pair of conditions to another.

Statistical software can check whether the variances of the difference scores are too different. We don’t need to get into the specifics here, but if you see the name “Mauchly’s test” in your output, it is one common check. Software will often also report a correction such as “Greenhouse–Geisser,” which reduces the degrees of freedom, increasing the critical \(F\) required to reject the null. Post-hoc tests using Tukey’s HSD are also sensitive to sphericity, because they rely on the single pooled \(MS_{\text{error}}\). If sphericity is questionable, a common alternative is to compare each pair of conditions with its own related-samples \(t\) test, using only that pair’s difference scores, with a stricter \(\alpha\) to make up for running several tests.

For our five-kid dataset, software would tell you that sphericity is acceptable, but with so few scores, changing one value or adding one more child could easily change that. As usual, the more data we have, the more reliable not only the estimates of means and stuff become, but also our assessment of the extent to which assumptions like this are met or violated.
