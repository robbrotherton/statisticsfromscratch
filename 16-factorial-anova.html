16  Factorial ANOVA

Four shaded normal curves in two alternating colors grow together, then separate.

Here, we’ll introduce the last variant of ANOVA that we’ll deal with: the factorial ANOVA, meaning ANOVA used with research designs that have more than one independent variable. This enables us to see not just the effect of a single variable, but how two variables interact. It’s slightly more complicated, both conceptually and mathematically, but it’s based on the same idea of partitioning variance that we’ve used in the previous two chapters.

Social facilitation or social impairment

We’ve talked about social facilitation in the context of Norman Triplett’s little flag racing machine. In Chapter 13 we met his “Competition Machine,” on which children turned a handle to race a little flag around a track, either by themselves or with a rival. I told you Triplett categorized some children as “stimulated positively” by competition—faster against a rival than alone. (I picked the data we analyzed in Chapters 13 and 15 from that group.) But other kids, Triplett noted, were “stimulated adversely.” They performed worse against the rival, becoming too amped up to perform well. I glossed over that before, but it’s worth grappling with. Why might some kids have been helped by the social element, while others were hindered?

Psychologist Robert Zajonc suggested an answer decades later, by expanding social facilitation theory to allow for both facilitation and impairment, depending on the nature of the task (Zajonc, 1965). His idea was that the audience might increase physiological arousal, getting you a bit amped up, just as Triplett noticed, and that being amped up makes us more likely to do whatever comes most naturally. Zajonc called this the dominant response. Whether that helps or hinders depends on the task. When a task is easy or well practiced, the dominant response is generally the right one, so an audience can improve performance. When the task is difficult or unfamiliar, the dominant response is often wrong, and the social pressure gets in the way. That might be what happened with Triplett’s kids: maybe cranking the handle came naturally to some of them, while others were still getting the hang of it.

With this in mind, imagine we set up an experiment in which we recruit some kids, like Triplett’s racers, but this time they’re given a printed maze—the kind that you find in kids’ activity books—and asked to draw a path to the exit as quickly as they can.

What makes a maze easy or difficult? Since the exit is on the right, the obvious thing to do at each choice point is to head toward it, turning right every time you can, and up or down as needed. That’s the dominant response. In the easy maze, it leads most directly to the exit. In the hard one, it doesn’t—you have to look ahead and overcome the urge to do the obvious thing. If Zajonc was right, having an audience should make kids more inclined to do the obvious thing, which will be good for performance on the easy maze, bad on the hard one.

So part of our study’s design is to randomly assign each kid an easy or a hard maze. But we also randomly assign them to complete their maze alone, or watched by an audience. This should allow us to test not just whether the presence of an audience affects performance, but whether the audience has a different effect depending on the difficulty of the task.

How does this new, more complicated design change how we analyze the data, and the conclusions we can draw? We turn once again to the logic of ANOVA, and partitioning variance. But unlike the previous chapter, where we divided the variability within groups into different components, this time we need to chop up the variability coming from the potential effects of the different factors.

Factorial designs

To refresh your memory of the terminology that goes with the ANOVA procedure, a factor defines the different groups being compared, usually an independent variable that the researcher manipulates either between or within participants. The levels are the individual conditions that make up a factor.

A factorial design, then, is one with more than one factor, each factor with two or more levels, with every combination of those levels represented. For our purposes, we’re only going to look at one kind of factorial design: the 2×2 (“two-by-two”) between-participants ANOVA, which has two between-participants factors, each with two levels. Designs can have more factors or levels, and can use within-participants factors or a mix of between- and within-participants factors (called a mixed-factorial design), but the basic logic remains the same.

Main effects, marginal means, and interactions

With a two-by-two design, we end up with four individual groups, one for each combination of the levels of each factor.1 So generically, let’s say we have Factor A and Factor B. If we arrange them in a table, you can see the four conditions this creates.

The extra row and column in Table 16.2 contain the marginal means: the mean of everyone at one level of a factor, ignoring the other factor. \(M_{A_1}\) is the mean of all the scores in the \(A_1\) column; \(M_{B_1}\) is the mean of all the scores in the \(B_1\) row. In the corner is the grand mean: the mean of every score, all put together in a single big group, as we have done before.

When it comes to the hypothesis test for a factorial design, we’ll actually run several hypothesis tests. For this 2×2 design we’ll have three separate tests.

First, there are the main effects, one per factor. The main effect of Factor A asks whether there is an overall difference between the levels of Factor A, ignoring the levels of Factor B. We just pool everyone together depending on which level of Factor A they were in, regardless of their level of Factor B. In other words, we compare the marginal means for Factor A. The main effect of Factor B is the same idea, comparing its marginal means while ignoring Factor A.

We’re also interested in the interaction between factors A and B. This is a major reason for using a design like this, instead of just two separate studies to look at Factors A and B in isolation. We want to know if Factor A and Factor B interact—that is, if the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other.

Our social facilitation idea predicts just such an interaction: being watched should help on the easy maze but hinder performance on the hard maze. In other words, the effect of the audience depends on the difficulty of the maze.

The interaction can be the most difficult part of this analysis to really grasp. There are different patterns of results that can reflect an interaction. We’ll come back to those after we run through the calculations and see the results, so we can talk it through more concretely.

Partitioning variance in factorial designs

The factorial ANOVA achieves all this the same way as the other ANOVAs we’ve seen, by partitioning variability in the data.

It starts out the same as every other ANOVA. We separate the total variability into variability between groups and variability within groups, each measured by its sum of squares. (Here each cell of the table is a group, so our 2×2 design has four individual groups.)

In Chapter 14, that was all the partitioning we could do. In Chapter 15, with related samples, we were able to divide up the variability within groups. Now, assuming we’re just using between-participants factors, we leave the variance within groups alone and divide up the variability between groups instead. The four group means might differ because of Factor A, because of Factor B, or because of the interaction between the two. By partitioning the treatment effects, we can test each of those components separately.

As before, the degrees of freedom partition just like the sums of squares. Dividing a \(SS\) value by its respective \(df\) gives a mean square. Eventually, each of the main effects and the interaction gets its own \(F\) ratio. Each has its own numerator, and all use the same denominator: the mean square within groups, our estimate of chance variability.

\[ \begin{aligned} F_A &= \frac{MS_A}{MS_{\text{within}}} \\[1.5ex] F_B &= \frac{MS_B}{MS_{\text{within}}} \\[1.5ex] F_{A \times B} &= \frac{MS_{A \times B}}{MS_{\text{within}}} \end{aligned} \]

Sums of squares and degrees of freedom

Arriving at those \(F\) ratios involves the same basic process as before. We start with between and within groups \(SS\) and \(df\). The equations are exactly the same as in Chapter 14. Remember that the design tells us the number of individual groups. For example, with a 2×2 design \(k = 4\).

\[ \begin{align} SS_{\text{total}} &= \Sigma (X - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ SS_{\text{within}} &= \Sigma (X - M_{\text{group}})^2 \\ SS_{\text{between}} &= \Sigma n_{\text{group}}(M_{\text{group}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \end{align} \]

\[ \begin{align} df_{\text{total}} &= N - 1 \\ df_{\text{within}} &= N - k \\ df_{\text{between}} &= k - 1 \end{align} \]

The new equations here are for the three pieces of the between-groups variability: the main effects and the interaction.2 Each main effect comes from regrouping the scores by one factor, ignoring the other.

Here \(M_A\) stands for each marginal mean for Factor A in turn, from the bottom margin of Table 16.2. We square each one’s deviation from the grand mean, multiply by \(n_A\), the number of scores at that level rather than in a single cell, and add those up. The same logic applies to Factor B, using the marginal means from the right-hand margin.

\[ \begin{align} SS_A &= \Sigma n_A(M_A - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ SS_B &= \Sigma n_B(M_B - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \end{align} \]

Notice that this is basically the same equation as for \(SS_{\text{between}}\), just with a different subscript. The only difference is that the means represent different groupings of scores—all the scores at each level of one factor combined, rather than all four groups separately. We also employed the same idea in Chapter 15, with \(SS_{\text{people}}\) = \(\Sigma k(M_{\text{person}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2\). In each case, we’re just grouping scores one way or another to isolate different sources of variability.

The degrees of freedom for each factor are its number of levels, \(k_A\) or \(k_B\), minus one.

\[ \begin{align} df_A &= k_A - 1 \\ df_B &= k_B - 1 \end{align} \]

The interaction

The interaction’s \(SS\) and \(df\) are simply whatever is left over: between-groups variability not already partitioned either into Factor A or Factor B. So we can find those through simple subtraction.

\[ \begin{align} SS_{A \times B} &= SS_{\text{between}} - SS_A - SS_B \\ df_{A \times B} &= df_{\text{between}} - df_A - df_B \end{align} \]

For a two-by-two design, that leaves \(3 - 1 - 1 = 1\) degree of freedom. More generally, it works out to \((k_A - 1)(k_B - 1)\), much like the error degrees of freedom in Chapter 15.

If you feel short-changed by the subtraction, we can also get the interaction variability directly from the data, which helps show what it represents. We give each group a prediction: the mean we’d expect for that group if the two main effects were all that was going on. To make the prediction, we start at the grand mean, then adjust for how far the mean for the group’s level of Factor A is from the grand mean, and again for its level of Factor B. That simplifies to \(M_A + M_B - M_{\text{grand}}\). In the table, \(M_A\) and \(M_B\) are the two marginal means in line with the group’s cell: the one at the bottom of its column and the one at the end of its row.

The interaction is about how far each group’s actual mean falls from that prediction. We square each of those differences and multiply by the number of scores in the group, just as we did for the between-groups sum of squares.

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{A \times B} = \Sigma n_{\text{group}}(&M_{\text{group}} - M_A \\ &{}- M_B + M_{\text{grand}})^2 \end{aligned} \]

If there were no interaction at all, every group mean would sit right on its prediction, and this would come to zero. The farther the group means are from where the main effects alone would put them, the bigger the interaction.

Factorial ANOVA in context

To make this more concrete, let’s return to our maze experiment. Maze difficulty is our Factor A and audience is our Factor B.

Table 16.3 has the mean time for each of the four groups, with the marginal means around the edges.

Kids who did the easy maze averaged 32 seconds and kids who did the hard maze averaged 48. Kids working alone averaged 38 seconds and kids being watched averaged 42. Each of those marginal means represents eight kids. And the grand mean, 40 seconds, is in the corner.

So the main effect of maze tells us about that 16-second difference between 32 and 48, and the main effect of audience is about the 4-second difference between 38 and 42. But even though watched kids were 4 seconds slower than solo kids overall, notice that they were 8 seconds faster than solo kids on the easy maze and 16 seconds slower on the hard one. That’s the kind of thing the interaction picks up. It’s only by comparing everything going on in the data—marginal means and individual group means—that we can get the full picture.

We’ll work through calculating the \(F\) ratios in a moment, but first let’s see how the various sums of squares are built up in this factorial analysis.

Here are our four groups of scores, colored by the combination of maze type and audience.

As before, total variability is the sum of squared deviations of every score from the grand mean.

Then \(SS_{\text{within}}\): the sum of squared deviations of each score from its own group mean.

Next, \(SS_{\text{between}}\): the sum of squared deviations of each group mean from the grand mean, multiplied by the number of scores in that group. We’ll split this into the main effects and interaction.

To isolate the maze component, we recolor the scores by maze type, ignoring the audience factor.

That gives us two groups of eight scores: everyone who did the easy maze and everyone who did the hard maze.

We square the deviations of the two marginal means from the grand mean, just as we did for the four individual group means.

We count each square eight times, once per score in these groups. This gives the \(SS_{\text{maze}}\) part of \(SS_{\text{between}}\).

Now we regroup by audience, ignoring the maze: eight scores from kids working alone and eight from kids being watched.

Again, square each marginal mean’s deviation from the grand mean and count it eight times. This gives the \(SS_{\text{audience}}\) part of \(SS_{\text{between}}\).

Now we can reset back to the 4 individual groups. There is one more unclaimed part of the between-groups variability. This represents variability due to the interaction between our factors. It’s whatever is left over after accounting for the main effects. We can find it through subtraction: \(SS_{\text{interaction}} = SS_{\text{between}} - SS_{\text{maze}} - SS_{\text{audience}}\). Job done. But let’s see what that means in a more concrete way.

To quantify the interaction directly, we first predict what the individual group means would be based only on the main effects. Starting from the grand mean, the maze effect shifts the predicted means 8 seconds down for the easy maze and 8 seconds up for the hard maze, since those easy and hard marginal means were 16 seconds apart.

The audience main effect shifts the predicted means 2 seconds up for the watched groups and 2 seconds down for the alone groups, since the watched and alone marginal means were 4 seconds apart. We add these adjustments to the maze prediction, so each group has a predicted mean shown as the dotted line. If there were no interaction at all, this is where the group means would be. That’s not quite the case here.

Now we compare each group’s actual mean with its predicted mean. Compare how? Deviations, of course! Find the deviations, square them, and add them up (counting each square \(n\) times). That gives the interaction sum of squares, the part of between-groups variability that can’t be explained by the main effects alone. It fills the empty portion of \(SS_{\text{between}}\) exactly.

We started with \(SS_{\text{total}}\), and split it into \(SS_{\text{within}}\) and \(SS_{\text{between}}\). Then we split \(SS_{\text{between}}\) into the three parts: \(SS_{\text{maze}}\), \(SS_{\text{audience}}\), and \(SS_{\text{interaction}}\).

The three split-up sums for the main effects and interaction get divided by their \(df\) (1 in each case) to become the three separate \(F\) ratio numerators. \(SS_{\text{within}}\) gets divided by its \(df\), and the resulting \(MS_{\text{within}}\) becomes the denominator for all three.

A factorial ANOVA, step by step

So now let’s work through a hypothesis test for this design, using the maze and audience example in Table 16.1.

Step 1: State the hypotheses

We need a null and alternative hypothesis for each of our three tests. For maze difficulty, the null is that the easy and hard mazes have equal population mean times, averaging across the audience conditions:

\[ H_0: \mu_{\text{easy}} = \mu_{\text{hard}} \]

For audience, the null is that the alone and watched conditions have equal population mean times, averaging across the mazes:

\[ H_0: \mu_{\text{alone}} = \mu_{\text{watched}} \]

Each alternative is that those means differ. For the interaction, the null is that the effect of being watched is the same on both mazes; the alternative is that it depends on the maze.

Step 2: Set the decision criteria

The critical values for the \(F\) ratios we’ll produce all come from the same \(F\) table as before. We need one for each test. We’ll use \(\alpha = .05\). Each factor has two levels, so each main effect has \(2 - 1 = 1\) degree of freedom in the numerator. The interaction also has one, which is what’s left of \(df_{\text{between}} = 3\) once the two main effects are taken out. And with \(N = 16\) scores in \(k = 4\) groups, the denominator has \(df_{\text{within}} = N - k = 12\).

Here all three tests have the same numerator and denominator degrees of freedom, so they share a critical value. In Table 16.4, the column for 1 and the row for 12 give a critical \(F\) of 4.75. We’ll reject the null for any of the three tests whose \(F\) exceeds that value. In a more complicated between-participants design, the numerator degrees of freedom can differ, although the denominator stays the same.

Now, remember how we went out of our way to control the experiment-wise error rate in the last couple of chapters, with a single omnibus test followed by a special post-hoc test for the multiple comparisons? Well, now we’re doing three \(F\) tests, each at \(\alpha = .05\). Are we holding the experiment-wise error rate steady at \(.05\)? Nope! The three tests’ error rates pile up in exactly the way we usually try to avoid. If nothing at all were going on with our mazes and audiences, the chance of at least one of the three tests coming out significant would be about 14%, just like the three \(t\) tests we worried about in Chapter 14. Why do we let that happen here? Just because! Somehow this became the norm. There are statistical solutions, but a review of published research found that researchers almost never used them (Cramer et al., 2016). We’ll go along with the convention for now, and come back to it when we interpret the results.

Step 3: Collect and analyze the data

As before, a summary table helps keep track of the calculations. Notice the new rows for Factor A, Factor B, and the interaction.

Here’s our means table again, now with each group’s sum of squared deviations to save you some calculation. From here on I’ll label the components with the factor’s name in place of the generic \(A\) and \(B\).

\[ \begin{gathered} N = 16 \qquad n = 4 \qquad k = 4 \\ k_{\text{maze}} = 2 \qquad k_{\text{audience}} = 2 \end{gathered} \]

First, the familiar components. For within groups, we can just add the four groups’ sums of squares from Table 16.6:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{within}} &= \Sigma (X - M_{\text{group}})^2 \\ &= 66 + 62 + 74 + 86 \\ &= 288 \end{aligned} \]

\[ df_{\text{within}} = N - k = 16 - 4 = 12 \]

For between groups, we find each group mean’s deviation from the grand mean, square it, and multiply by the four scores it represents:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{between}} &= \Sigma n_{\text{group}}(M_{\text{group}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ &= 4(-4)^2 + 4(0)^2 \\ &\quad {}+ 4(-12)^2 + 4(16)^2 \\ &= 1{,}664 \end{aligned} \]

\[ df_{\text{between}} = k - 1 = 4 - 1 = 3 \]

Those two add up to the total: \(SS_{\text{total}} = 1{,}664 + 288 = 1{,}952\), with \(df_{\text{total}} = N - 1 = 15\).

Now for the new components. For the maze factor, we use the two maze means from the bottom margin of the table, 32 and 48 seconds. Each one represents eight scores:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{maze}} &= \Sigma n_{\text{maze}}(M_{\text{maze}} - M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ &= 8(32 - 40)^2 + 8(48 - 40)^2 \\ &= 1{,}024 \end{aligned} \]

\[ df_{\text{maze}} = k_{\text{maze}} - 1 = 2 - 1 = 1 \]

For the audience factor, we do the same with the two audience means from the right-hand margin, 38 and 42 seconds:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{audience}} &= \Sigma n_{\text{audience}}(M_{\text{audience}} \\ &\qquad {}- M_{\text{grand}})^2 \\ &= 8(38 - 40)^2 + 8(42 - 40)^2 \\ &= 64 \end{aligned} \]

\[ df_{\text{audience}} = k_{\text{audience}} - 1 = 2 - 1 = 1 \]

Then we can find the interaction by subtracting both of those from the between-groups component:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{interaction}} &= SS_{\text{between}} - SS_{\text{maze}} \\ &\quad {}- SS_{\text{audience}} \\ &= 1{,}664 - 1{,}024 - 64 \\ &= 576 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} df_{\text{interaction}} &= df_{\text{between}} - df_{\text{maze}} \\ &\quad {}- df_{\text{audience}} \\ &= 3 - 1 - 1 = 1 \end{aligned} \]

But let’s also see how the interaction builds up from the group means. Take the kids who did the easy maze alone. The mean for the easy maze is 32 and the mean for working alone is 38, so the prediction is \(32 + 38 - 40 = 30\) seconds. Their actual mean was 36 seconds: 6 seconds slower than the main effects alone would suggest. Table 16.7 does the same for all four groups.

Every group mean is 6 seconds from its prediction, in one direction or the other. (The four differences always come out the same size with a two-by-two design with equal group sizes.) Squaring each difference and multiplying by the four scores in each group gives the same answer as before:

\[ \begin{aligned} SS_{\text{interaction}} &= 4(6)^2 + 4(-6)^2 \\ &\quad {}+ 4(-6)^2 + 4(6)^2 \\ &= 576 \end{aligned} \]

Now we have all the sums of squares and their degrees of freedom. Dividing each one by its degrees of freedom gives the mean squares:

\[ \begin{align} MS_{\text{maze}} &= \frac{SS_{\text{maze}}}{df_{\text{maze}}} = \frac{1{,}024}{1} = 1{,}024 \\ MS_{\text{audience}} &= \frac{SS_{\text{audience}}}{df_{\text{audience}}} = \frac{64}{1} = 64 \\ MS_{\text{interaction}} &= \frac{SS_{\text{interaction}}}{df_{\text{interaction}}} = \frac{576}{1} = 576 \\ MS_{\text{within}} &= \frac{SS_{\text{within}}}{df_{\text{within}}} = \frac{288}{12} = 24 \end{align} \]

Finally, we divide each of the three new mean squares by the within-groups mean square:

\[ \begin{align} F_{\text{maze}} &= \frac{MS_{\text{maze}}}{MS_{\text{within}}} = \frac{1{,}024}{24} \approx 42.67 \\ F_{\text{audience}} &= \frac{MS_{\text{audience}}}{MS_{\text{within}}} = \frac{64}{24} \approx 2.67 \\ F_{\text{interaction}} &= \frac{MS_{\text{interaction}}}{MS_{\text{within}}} = \frac{576}{24} = 24.00 \end{align} \]

Example answer

\(F_{\text{maze}} = 1{,}600/24 = 66.67\) and \(F_{\text{audience}} = 144/24 = 6.00\). Both exceed 4.75, and so does the interaction’s 10.67, so all three effects are significant. Step 4: Make a decision

The maze and interaction \(F\) ratios both exceed the critical value of 4.75, so we reject those two null hypotheses. The audience \(F\) does not, so we fail to reject its null.

Kids took significantly longer on the hard maze overall. The average difference between being watched and working alone wasn’t significant. But the significant interaction tells us that this average doesn’t tell the whole story: the effect of being watched depends on which maze the kids did.

Step 5: Estimate the effect and follow up

Effect size: partial \(\eta^2\)

We also estimate an effect size for each test, whether or not it was significant. As in Chapter 15, we’ll use partial eta squared: one for maze, one for audience, and one for the interaction.

Each numerator is that effect’s \(SS\), and each denominator contains just that effect’s \(SS\) plus \(SS_{\text{within}}\). For maze, we set aside the \(SS\) for audience and for the interaction, just as we dumped \(SS_{\text{people}}\) out of the bucket in Chapter 15. We ask what proportion of what’s left is \(SS_{\text{maze}}\). The same rule applies to audience and the interaction.

\[ \begin{align} &\text{Maze:} \\[0.5ex] &\quad \eta^2_p = \frac{SS_{\text{maze}}}{SS_{\text{maze}} + SS_{\text{within}}} \\[0.5ex] &\quad \phantom{\eta^2_p} = \frac{1{,}024}{1{,}024 + 288} = .78 \\[2ex] &\text{Audience:} \\[0.5ex] &\quad \eta^2_p = \frac{SS_{\text{audience}}}{SS_{\text{audience}} + SS_{\text{within}}} \\[0.5ex] &\quad \phantom{\eta^2_p} = \frac{64}{64 + 288} = .18 \\[2ex] &\text{Interaction:} \\[0.5ex] &\quad \eta^2_p = \frac{SS_{\text{interaction}}}{SS_{\text{interaction}} + SS_{\text{within}}} \\[0.5ex] &\quad \phantom{\eta^2_p} = \frac{576}{576 + 288} = .67 \end{align} \]

These three proportions have different denominators, so they aren’t shares of one total and don’t need to add up to 1.

Simple effects

In the last two chapters, we followed a significant \(F\) with post-hoc tests. We don’t need them for our main effects: each factor only has two levels, so if there’s a difference, there’s only one place it can be.3 The interaction does need some additional probing, though. It tells us that the effect of being watched isn’t the same on both mazes, but not what the effect is on either one. Watched kids were 8 seconds faster than kids working alone on the easy maze, and 16 seconds slower on the hard maze. Is each of those a reliable difference in its own right?

To find out, we test simple effects: the effect of one factor at a single level of the other. Here that means comparing the alone and watched means twice, once for the easy maze and once for the hard maze. Mathematically, there’s nothing new. Each simple effect is just a between-groups sum of squares for two of our four groups: the means of 36 and 28 seconds around the easy-maze mean of 32, and the means of 40 and 56 around the hard-maze mean of 48. Two means give \(df = 1\), so each mean square is the same as its sum of squares. And we divide by the same \(MS_{\text{within}}\) as before, since it’s still our best estimate of chance variability, based on all sixteen kids.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Easy maze:} \\ &\quad SS = 4(36 - 32)^2 + 4(28 - 32)^2 = 128 \\ &\quad F = \frac{128}{24} \approx 5.33 \\[1.5ex] &\text{Hard maze:} \\ &\quad SS = 4(40 - 48)^2 + 4(56 - 48)^2 = 512 \\ &\quad F = \frac{512}{24} \approx 21.33 \end{aligned} \]

With \(df = 1\) and \(12\), the critical value is the same 4.75 as before, and both \(F\) ratios are beyond it.4 So being watched made kids significantly faster on the easy maze, and significantly slower on the hard maze. Both halves of the pattern are significant at the unadjusted .05 threshold.

We could have come at the interaction the other way, testing the effect of the maze for kids working alone and for kids being watched. But that wouldn’t directly test our predictions of facilitation on the easy task and impairment on the hard one, and it would require two more tests, each with its own error rate. We could adjust alpha for multiple comparisons. But we don’t necessarily have to test every possible simple effect; we can use some restraint and let the research question choose which to test.

Statistical power

The logic of power is the same as in Activity 14.7, but here we need to plan for the effect we’re interested in detecting. A study can have enough power to detect a main effect and still miss an interaction. For our question, we’d plan the sample size around how much the audience effect might differ between the two mazes, along with the variability within groups and our chosen alpha. Four kids per cell make a manageable teaching example; real studies generally need more.

Reporting and interpreting results

Reporting results

We report the design, what we measured, and the \(F\) ratios, degrees of freedom, \(p\) values, and effect sizes for all three tests, plus the simple effects. The four group means are in Table 16.6 and Figure 16.4.

To examine the influence of maze difficulty and an audience on performance, a 2-factor ANOVA was conducted with completion times as the dependent variable and Maze Difficulty and Audience as between-participants independent variables. There was no significant main effect of Audience (\(F(1, 12) = 2.67\), \(p = .128\), \(\eta^2_p = .18\)). There was, however, a significant main effect of Maze Difficulty (\(F(1, 12) = 42.67\), \(p < .001\), \(\eta^2_p = .78\)); overall, times were longer on the hard maze. Moreover, there was a significant interaction between Maze Difficulty and Audience (\(F(1, 12) = 24.00\), \(p < .001\), \(\eta^2_p = .67\)); the effect of being watched depended on maze difficulty. Unadjusted simple-effects tests showed faster times when watched on the easy maze (\(F(1, 12) = 5.33\), \(p = .040\)) and slower times when watched on the hard maze (\(F(1, 12) = 21.33\), \(p < .001\)).

Either bar graphs or line graphs can show the group means. Line graphs are often preferred for interactions, because the lines make it easy to see whether the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. One factor becomes ticks on the x-axis, and the other factor is represented by a line for each of its levels, possibly with different dash types or colors, and even different shapes for the points, as in Figure 16.4. There I’ve drawn the same data two different ways: in one, Maze type (easy vs. hard) is on the x-axis with Audience (alone vs. watched) as the line type; in the other the factors are swapped. The means and interaction are exactly the same in both graphs; the choice of which factor to map to which axis just changes the shape, with the lines crossing in one and not in the other. We wouldn’t include both graphs in a report; we’d choose the one we think best shows the pattern of interest.

I said earlier that understanding the idea of an interaction can be tricky. Seeing the findings represented as a line graph can help a lot, when you know what to look for. Our results showed an interaction, though it looked a bit different in the two graph variations. Social facilitation

So what do our findings say about social facilitation theory? Our made-up kids showed the predicted pattern: facilitation on the easy task and impairment on the hard one. Because we said we randomly assigned kids to conditions, we could take the findings as evidence of causality—that being watched causes the different performance depending on maze type. Obviously with the tiny sample sizes, we wouldn’t be able to draw reliable conclusions even if the data was real. (Like I said, it wasn’t: I just made it up.)

The pattern fits with real social facilitation research on people, generally speaking. A review of 241 studies found that real people broadly showed the predicted pattern of facilitation or impairment depending on task type, though the effects tend to be small (Bond & Titus, 1983).

I am conspicuously emphasizing research on people there, because I feel like there’s something I should confess. The maze idea? It was inspired by one of Robert Zajonc’s studies. But in the real study, it wasn’t kids doing the mazes. It was cockroaches. Yeah. I didn’t want to spend a whole chapter talking about cockroaches, so I made you picture delightful little kids instead. You’re welcome. But if you’re interested to read about what kind of mazes cockroaches find easy or hard, and how they respond to having an audience of other cockroaches watching on, be my guest (Zajonc et al., 1969).

The cockroach evidence hasn’t held up so well, though. A more recent preregistered replication found that an audience slowed the roaches down on both tasks: two significant main effects, but no significant interaction (Halfmann et al., 2020). Speaking of the interaction, Halfmann et al. pointed out that Zajonc and his colleagues hadn’t actually tested it, even though it was the critical prediction of social facilitation theory. Our central question was about the interaction too, but the factorial ANOVA gives us three bundled tests—the main effects in addition to the interaction. I mentioned that this can inflate the Type I error rate, but not if we commit to just basing our conclusions on one specific test in advance, for example by preregistering our analysis plan. If only the interaction matters, we can just ignore the main effects. If we’d committed to that one test up front and used it alone to judge that prediction, its Type I error rate would be .05 (see Cramer et al., 2016).

Assumptions

The assumptions are the same as in Chapter 14, applied to all four cells: independent observations, approximately normal population distributions within each cell, and equal population variances across cells. The equal-variance assumption is also what lets us use the pooled \(MS_{\text{within}}\) for the simple-effects tests.
