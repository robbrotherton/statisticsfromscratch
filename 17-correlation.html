17  Correlation

A grid of nonoverlapping multicolored dots pops into view, aligns at perfect positive correlation, then moves smoothly to a correlation of about 0.6.

In this chapter and the next, we’ll cover the techniques of correlation and regression. Whereas the \(z\), \(t\), and ANOVA statistics we’ve covered so far deal with examining differences between means, correlation and regression focus on associations between variables. As we’ll see, though, this isn’t as big a difference as you might expect. Correlation and regression are just further extensions of the basic idea of variability that has powered all of our inferential statistics so far.

Buying happiness

In Chapter 12, we imagined giving people $10 and telling them to spend it either on themselves or on someone else. I based that idea on a real experiment, in which people randomly assigned to spend a small amount of cash on someone else reported being happier later that day than people assigned to spend it on themselves (Dunn et al., 2008). At the end of the chapter I made a big to-do about how one study is never the final word on anything.

Well, the researchers actually did more than one kind of study. In the same paper, they reported another in which they didn’t give out cash or assign anyone to anything. Instead they just surveyed a representative sample of 632 Americans, asking how happy they were in general and how much they spent in a typical month on gifts for other people and donations to charity.

How might we analyze the data to see whether spending on others goes with being happier? We don’t really have groups to compare in this case, just two answers from each person. I suppose we could make some groups. We could split the sample into people who spent something on others and people who spent nothing, and compare the two groups’ mean happiness with an independent-samples \(t\) test.

But that would throw away a lot of good information. Everyone who spent anything gets lumped together, so we lose any distinction between someone who spent $5 on others and someone who spent $500. There has to be a better way! How might we make use of each person’s actual amount spent?

The idea Quiz 17.1 was getting at is to look at each person’s answers together, not to find a difference between them (like the related-samples \(t\) test did), but to find an association. Take someone who spent more than average on other people. If that person was also happier than average, their two deviations point in the same direction. How about someone who was both a miser and miserable?1 Their deviations point in the same direction too.

Here’s what that idea looks like with some data. (These aren’t the real survey responses, just a handful of made-up people to illustrate.)

People in the other two quarters are above average on one variable and below average on the other (the hollow dots). If you just count them up, 15 people are on the same side and 5 are on opposite sides. The two variables tend to go together.

Counting dots in the four sections like this is a rough version of what a correlation does. The real math also takes into account precisely how far each person is from the two means, so that someone far above average on both counts for more than someone only just above. And just as with a difference between group means, we’ll need to know whether an apparent pattern is more than we’d expect by chance.

What would it mean?

Now I know you’re excited to do the math, and we’ll get to it soon. But first, let’s distinguish two closely related terms. A correlation coefficient is the number we’ll calculate to quantify how two variables go together. Correlational also describes a research design. If the researchers had created two groups by letting people choose to spend $10 on themselves or someone else, instead of randomly assigning them, they could have analyzed the data using a \(t\) test rather than a correlation coefficient. But it would be a correlational design nonetheless. If the people who spent their money on someone else were happier, would that tell us the same thing as finding that people randomly assigned to do so were happier?

In the actual survey, people who spent more on others did tend to be happier. Can we conclude that spending on others makes people happier? Not necessarily. Maybe being happier makes people more generous. Or maybe there’s some other explanation. Perhaps people with higher incomes could afford to give more and also tended to be happier. The researchers anticipated that possibility and asked about income, too. Taking it into account (as we’ll learn how to do later in the chapter) barely changed the association.2

That makes income less convincing as a potential explanation, but I’m sure you can think of others. The researchers hadn’t measured everything that might make people both more generous and happier. As Dunn and colleagues put it themselves, “the correlational nature of this design precludes causal inferences.”

That’s why they did the experiment, giving people some money and telling them who to spend it on. But experiments have disadvantages of their own. In everyday life, people aren’t usually handed an envelope of cash and told how to spend it. An experiment can give a causal answer to a narrow, somewhat contrived question. The survey answers a more realistic question, less conclusively. Together they contribute to a body of evidence, but neither is definitive on its own.

The correlation coefficient

Now we can get into the details of the correlation statistic itself. The statistic we’ll use is called Pearson’s \(r\) correlation coefficient, named after Karl Pearson—the statistician Gosset went to study with in Chapter 11. But its development also involved another familiar figure: Francis Galton, whose board of bouncing balls helped us think about sampling in Chapter 7.

Galton was interested in how people’s physical characteristics varied together. Taller people tend to have bigger feet, for example, but not always. How could you put a number on the consistency of the relationship between measurements? Galton had already used the letter \(r\) in his work on regression, the closely related technique we’ll meet in the next chapter. But Pearson developed the mathematical treatment behind the coefficient we use today (Pearson, 1896), which is why it bears his name.

Characteristics

The correlation coefficient is a single number that quantifies the strength and direction of a linear association between two quantitative variables.3 Because of how it’s calculated (as we’ll see shortly), \(r\) can range from \(-1\) to \(1\).

The absolute value quantifies the strength of the relationship, or, in other words, the consistency of the association between the variables. The stronger and more consistent their association, the closer the correlation will be to 1 (or \(-1\)). The weaker and less consistent their relationship, the closer the correlation will be to 0.

The sign, positive or negative, indicates the direction of the correlation. A positive correlation is one for which increases in one variable are associated with increases in the other—or, correspondingly, lower scores on one are associated with lower scores on the other. A negative correlation means that higher scores on one variable are associated with lower scores on the other—that they move in opposite directions.

These characteristics are illustrated in the scatterplots in Figure 17.1.

At zero, the ellipse in Figure 17.1 is basically a circle, with no upward or downward tendency, meaning that there is no consistent linear relationship between the two variables. As the correlation gets stronger, the ellipse gets longer and thinner. A “perfect correlation” of either \(1\) or \(-1\) means that every change in one variable is accompanied by a proportional change in the other; the dots fall into a straight line, and the ellipse becomes a line. In a sense, the correlation coefficient is a measure of how closely the data points follow a straight-line pattern.4

Calculating \(r\)

Now let’s see how to calculate Pearson’s \(r\). It’s based on the ideas of variability and covariability.

Here’s the equation for Pearson’s \(r\):

\[ r = \frac{SP}{\sqrt{SS_X SS_Y}} \tag{17.1}\]

\(SS_X\) and \(SS_Y\) should look familiar. \(SS\), as usual, refers to the sum of squared deviations, a measure of how scores vary around their mean. So here we have the separate sums of squares for \(X\) and \(Y\) getting combined in the denominator.

The numerator is a new quantity, \(SP\), the sum of products. This quantifies the covariability of \(X\) and \(Y\): the extent to which changes in \(X\) are associated with corresponding changes in \(Y\).

Remember that to calculate \(SS\), we square the deviation scores. And another way of saying that we square something is to say we multiply it by itself. So here are two equivalent ways of writing the equation for sum of squares:

\[ SS = \Sigma(X - M)^2 \]

\[ SS = \Sigma(X - M)(X - M) \]

The reason I point this out is that \(SP\) does something quite similar, but instead of multiplying deviations by themselves, like \(SS\) does, we multiply them by one another, to get the product of the two deviations. Then we sum those products to get the sum of products, \(SP\).

\[ SP = \Sigma(X - M_X)(Y - M_Y) \tag{17.2}\]

With \(SS\) we ask how far the scores on one variable spread around their mean. Some are below their mean, some above, but the squaring makes all the resulting values positive. With \(SP\), in contrast, the products can be positive or negative. Above average on both? Positive. Below average on both? Also a positive product, because we’ll be multiplying two negative deviations. Above on one and below on the other? That will make for a negative product.

So the numerator of Equation 17.1 measures how much \(X\) and \(Y\) vary together, and it can be positive or negative. The denominator combines how much each one varies on its own. Those were squared, so the denominator is always positive, and it requires the square root to put things back into the same units as the numerator.

If there were a perfect correlation, with every point on a straight line, then every deviation on \(X\) would be matched by a proportional deviation on \(Y\). All the variability in \(X\) would be matched by the variability in \(Y\), and vice versa. Those separate variabilities in the denominator would be equal to the covariability in the numerator. This is why \(r\) is bounded between \(-1\) and \(1\): the numerator can be equal to the denominator (or its negative), but its absolute value can never be greater. The more variability that isn’t shared, the smaller \(SP\) gets relative to the denominator, and the closer \(r\) gets to zero.

Worked example

Let’s work through a simple example to see how the sum of products is calculated. Suppose we’re interested in how the number of cups of coffee a student drinks on the morning of a test is related to their score. Maybe the caffeine will increase alertness and cognitive performance, leading to better test scores. We have data for seven students, as shown in Table 17.1.

Before we work through the full calculation, let’s picture the two sums of squares and the sum of products for this little dataset.

The coffee and test score scatterplot with dashed lines at the two means, 1 cup and a score of 70. Each student's product of deviations is drawn as a rectangle between their point and the place where the mean lines cross: −20 for student A, 5 for B, −7.5 for C, 0 for D, −10 for E, −2.5 for F, and 5 for G. They sum to SP = −30.

Here are our seven students again, with cups of coffee on the \(X\) axis and test score on the \(Y\) axis.

First we need the two means. The dashed lines mark the mean cups of coffee, \(M_X = 1\), and the mean test score, \(M_Y = 70\). Between them they split the plot into four quarters.

Start with coffee consumption. Each horizontal deviation from \(M_X = 1\) becomes the side of a square. Add the seven squared deviations: \(SS_X = 1 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 1 = 3\).

Now do the same for test scores. Each vertical deviation from \(M_Y = 70\) becomes the side of a square. Add the seven squared deviations: \(SS_Y = 400 + 100 + 225 + 25 + 400 + 25 + 25 = 1200\).

Each student has two deviations: how far they are from the mean on \(X\), and how far they are from the mean on \(Y\). Student A drank 1 cup less than average and scored 20 points above average. Student G is above average on both.

Now we multiply each student’s two deviations. The product is the area of the resulting rectangle, one deviation wide and the other deviation tall. The dots labeled A, C, E, and F are above one mean and below the other, so their deviations have opposite signs and their products are negative. Those are the orange rectangles with dashed outlines. Points B and G are on the same side of both means, so their products are positive. D is exactly at the mean for coffee, so its product is zero.

Add up the products and we have the sum of products: \(SP = -20 + 5 - 7.5 + 0 - 10 - 2.5 + 5 = -30\). The negative products outweigh the positive ones, so \(SP\) comes out negative.

If you’re working by hand, Table 17.2 is Chapter 4’s deviations table extended to include deviations and squared deviations for both variables. There’s just one new column, for those products. To make it, we multiply each row’s two deviations to get the product, then sum up that column to get \(SP\).

That gives us everything we need to calculate the correlation coefficient:

\[r = \frac{SP}{\sqrt{SS_X SS_Y}} = \frac{-30}{\sqrt{3 \times 1200}} = \frac{-30}{60} = -.50\]

A correlation test, step by step

So we’ve calculated a correlation coefficient, \(r\), for our little coffee consumption and test performance data set. The correlation came out negative: drinking more coffee was associated with lower test scores. But is that a significant relationship, or just due to chance? To know that, we need to submit the data to a full hypothesis test, following the same basic procedure we’ve used for every other hypothesis test.

Step 1: State the hypotheses

We use \(r\) for the sample correlation and the Greek letter \(\rho\) (rho) for the population correlation. The null hypothesis sets \(\rho\) to zero. Our nondirectional alternative allows for a linear association in either direction.

\[ H_0: \rho = 0 \]

\[ H_1: \rho \ne 0 \]

So the correlation hypothesis test asks, how likely is it that a correlation as strong as the one we observed would occur if there were no association in the population?

Step 2: Set the decision criteria

This is going to sound weird, but the test statistic for a correlation is actually a \(t\) statistic. Pearson’s \(r\) describes the size of the association in our sample, not how probable it is under the null hypothesis. So to test the null, we turn the \(r\) into a \(t\). We’ll see how in the next step, but for now, we need its degrees of freedom to find the critical \(t\). Its degrees of freedom are \(n - 2\), where \(n\) counts the pairs. Seven students give us seven observations of the relationship, even though we’ve recorded fourteen scores.

Why subtract two? Well, picture just two points on a scatterplot. We wouldn’t need an ellipse around them; we could draw a straight line passing through both, fitting perfectly. The correlation would be one or negative one. There’d be no opportunity for either point to deviate from the line, because those two points determine the line itself. It’s only when we add more observations that we can see any scatter around it. So fitting the line uses up two degrees of freedom, leaving \(n - 2\) deviations free to vary.5

So with seven pairs of scores in our data set, we have \(df = 5\). For our two-tailed test at \(\alpha = .05\), the critical values are \(\pm 2.571\).

Step 3: Collect and analyze the data

We already found \(r = -.50\) (Table 17.2). To test our hypothesis, we need to convert that into a \(t\) statistic. Remember the basic structure of a \(t\) statistic (Equation 12.1)? We subtract a population parameter defined by the null hypothesis from our sample statistic, then divide by a standard error. Here the null says \(\rho = 0\), and our sample statistic is \(r\). The denominator is the standard error of the correlation, \(s_r\).

\[ t = \frac{r - \rho}{s_r} \tag{17.3}\]

The equation for the standard error of the correlation is a little different from the ones we’ve seen before. It depends on the correlation itself, as well as the number of pairs of scores.

\[ s_r = \sqrt{\frac{1 - r^2}{n - 2}} \tag{17.4}\]

Remember how \(\eta^2\) in Chapter 14 told us what proportion of the total variability was accounted for by differences between the groups? \(r^2\) does a similar job for a linear relationship: it tells us the proportion of the variability in one variable that’s shared with the other. For our coffee data, \(r^2 = (-.50)^2 = .25\). So 25% of the variability in test scores is shared with coffee consumption, leaving 75% unaccounted for by that relationship. That’s the \(1 - r^2\) in the numerator.

So the standard error reflects variability left over after accounting for the predictable relationship, divided by the degrees of freedom, with a square root to undo the squaring. A stronger correlation makes the standard error smaller, because there is more predictability and less variability unaccounted for. As with the other standard errors we’ve seen, more observations make it smaller too, because more data gives us a more precise estimate.

For our coffee data, that works out like this:

\[t = \frac{r - \rho}{s_r} = \frac{-.50 - 0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{1 - (-.50)^2}{7 - 2}}} = \frac{-.50}{.387} = -1.29\]

Step 4: Make a decision

Our \(t = -1.29\) falls between the two critical values, so we fail to reject the null hypothesis.

The corresponding two-tailed \(p\)-value is approximately \(.25\), well above our \(\alpha\) of \(.05\).

Reporting and interpreting the results

Reporting results

As always, we need to report the results of our hypothesis test. Here, that statement will include the value of the correlation coefficient, Pearson’s \(r\), its degrees of freedom in parentheses, and the \(p\)-value for the correlation.

Drinking more coffee was associated with lower test scores, but the correlation did not reach statistical significance; \(r(5) = -.50\), \(p = .25\).

Interpreting the results

So what can we make of our result? We failed to reject the null hypothesis, but that doesn’t establish that there’s no relationship. We only have seven pairs of scores. Our \(r = -.50\) describes a fairly strong negative association in this sample, but with so few observations, it isn’t strong enough evidence to reject the null.

On the other hand, a weak correlation can be statistically significant, if it comes from a large enough sample. Suppose we increased the sample for our study up to \(n = 300\). In the larger sample, the correlation between coffee and test score is only \(-.15\). With \(df = 298\), the critical values are \(\pm 1.968\). Converting \(r = -.15\) gives \(t = -2.62\), beyond the critical value, so this smaller correlation would reach statistical significance, \(r(298) = -.15\), \(p = .009\). In fact, we could convert the critical \(t\) to an \(r\), which would tell us that with 300 pairs, any correlation stronger than about \(\pm .11\) would be significant. So whether a correlation is significant and how strong it is are separate questions.

That significance decision comes down to a binary threshold, but how should we interpret the correlation as a measure of effect size? How impressive is a correlation of \(.15\)? What about \(.05\), or \(.30\)? Cohen called \(r = .30\) medium (Cohen, 1988). Funder and Ozer, whose argument about small effects adding up we met in Chapter 9, call it large in psychological research (Funder & Ozer, 2019). In the social sciences, the behaviors or attitudes or whatever we’re interested in are generally multiply determined. There are a lot of reasons why you get a particular score on a particular exam, not just how many cups of coffee you drank or how many hours of sleep you got. Those things might just reveal a small piece of the puzzle. And a small piece can be worth knowing about, even if it doesn’t look terribly impressive on a scatterplot (Figure 17.3).

Correlation and causation

In the spending survey, the researchers asked whether income could account for the relationship between giving and happiness. But what does “taking income into account” actually involve? We can see the basic idea using nothing more than correlations.

Think back to our coffee and test score example. We found a negative correlation between coffee consumption and test scores, but we don’t know whether drinking coffee is causing lower test scores, or whether students who expect to do poorly on the exam are drinking more coffee, or whether some other factor is behind both. Maybe students who haven’t been sleeping are both drinking more coffee and scoring lower on the exam.

So suppose the study also recorded each student’s average nightly sleep over the week before the exam. (That sleep came before the morning’s coffee, so the coffee can’t have caused it.) Here are our seven students again, with their sleep. (We’ll use all 300 students for the correlations, but this shows what the data look like with a third variable.)

With three variables we can calculate three correlations, one for each pair. Table 17.5 shows them for all 300 students in what’s called a correlation matrix. Pick a row and a column and read across and down to where they meet. We’ve only filled in the lower half because correlating sleep with coffee gives us exactly the same number as correlating coffee with sleep.

Students who slept less tended to drink more coffee (\(r = -.35\)) and get lower test scores (\(r = .30\)). So how much of the relationship between coffee and test score is still there once we take sleep into account?

Partial correlations

If we’ve measured all three things, we can answer that question with a partial correlation. Think of two students who got the same amount of sleep. Would knowing which one drank more coffee still tell us anything about who scored higher? For each student, we work out how much more or less coffee they drank than we’d predict from their sleep. Then we do the same for their test score. The partial correlation is the correlation between those two sets of deviations. We’ll use this technique of measuring deviations from a prediction in the next chapter, but for now, we can calculate a partial correlation directly from the three correlation coefficients we already have. Here \(X\) is coffee, \(Y\) is test score, and \(Z\) is sleep.

\[ r_{XY \cdot Z} = \frac{r_{XY} - r_{XZ}\,r_{YZ}}{\sqrt{(1 - r_{XZ}^2)(1 - r_{YZ}^2)}} \tag{17.5}\]

The \(r_{XY \cdot Z}\) notation indicates we’re producing a correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\), controlling for \(Z\). The numerator subtracts covariability associated with sleep—sleep’s joint association with coffee and test scores. In the denominator, each \((1-r^2)\) is the fraction of variability left after accounting for sleep, first for coffee and then for test scores. Combining those fractions puts the result on the same \(−1\) to \(1\) scale as a regular correlation coefficient.

\[ r_{XY \cdot Z} = \frac{-.15 - (-.35)(.30)}{\sqrt{(1 - (-.35)^2)(1 - .30^2)}} = \frac{-.045}{.894} = -.05 \]

Accounting for sleep brings the correlation much closer to zero, from \(-.15\) to \(-.05\). Compare that with the spending survey at the beginning of the chapter. There, taking income into account barely changed the association between spending on others and happiness.

We can test a partial correlation by converting it to \(t\) the same way as before, except that we lose one more degree of freedom for each variable we account for, so here \(df = n - 3 = 297\). For the full sample of 300, that gives \(t = -0.87\), well short of the critical values of \(\pm 1.968\), so there’s no longer a significant association between coffee and test score, once we account for sleep. But we might also be interested in a slightly different question: how much did the association change? That would be a separate test, distinct from the significance of the partial correlation itself. We won’t go into the details here; I just want you to recognize the general idea if you come across it in research papers or whatever.

Looking at partial correlations, particularly when we set out to do our research with a specific third variable in mind, can add valuable context to our understanding of the relationships between variables. But it still doesn’t establish causation or rule out other explanations. Other unmeasured factors could still be influencing the relationship. That brings us back to the advantage of random assignment in Chapter 12. It should mean that all the other influences on the thing we’re interested in get evenly distributed across groups. It doesn’t make the effect of sleep or whatever else might be important go away; it just averages them out so that any remaining difference between groups is due to the variable we manipulated (or to chance, which is what the significance test is for).

Correlation assumptions

With correlational data, we have pairs of scores from the same person, and we’re interested in whether those scores are related. As with the related-samples analyses we’ve used, the assumption of independence applies between people, not between the two scores within a pair.

For the significance test we’ve used, we also assume the pairs of scores come from a bivariate normal distribution. With the \(t\) test and ANOVA we assumed scores came from a normally distributed population. With two variables being considered together, both populations should be normal, and at any given value of \(X\), the \(Y\) scores should be normally distributed. Visually, the cloud of dots on a scatterplot should be roughly elliptical, densest in the middle and thinning out smoothly toward the edges. The test can tolerate some departures from bivariate normality, though seven pairs tell us little about the shape of the population.

Lastly, when I defined Pearson’s \(r\), I said it measures the strength of linear association: how consistently the points follow a straight line. So if we calculate Pearson’s correlation coefficient, we’re assuming linearity.

It’s important to check, because things can be related in ways that a straight line doesn’t capture. Maybe the relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\) starts out with \(Y\) increasing as \(X\) increases, but changes direction at some point: as \(X\) keeps increasing, \(Y\) starts to decrease. (The first scoop of ice cream might improve your mood; by the fifth you might start feeling worse.) Or maybe \(X\) and \(Y\) keep increasing together, but the rate of increase changes at some point. Figure 17.6 illustrates some predictable (though increasingly contrived) relationships that Pearson’s \(r\) fails to capture. This is why it’s worth looking at the scatterplot before trying to summarize the relationship with a single number.

The number in parentheses is the degrees of freedom, not the sample size. For a correlation, \(df = n - 2\), so \(n = 48 + 2 = 50\) pairs.

In a large survey, the correlation between coffee drinking and test scores is statistically significant, \(p < .001\). Which conclusion does that support?

Response options

Drinking coffee lowers test scores

Doing badly on tests leads people to drink more coffee

No third variable is responsible for the relationship

A result this strong is unlikely under the null hypothesis

Answer

A result this strong is unlikely under the null hypothesis

Explanation

The significance test addresses chance, and only chance. A very small \(p\)-value makes it hard to believe there’s no association in the population, but it says nothing about why the two variables go together.

9

Think back to our seven students, with coffee consumption (\(x\)), test score (\(y\)), and hours of sleep (\(z\)). For just those seven students, \(r_{xy} = -.50\), \(r_{xz} = -.667\), and \(r_{yz} = .667\). What is \(r_{xy.z}\)?

Answer

-0.1

Explanation

\(r_{xy.z} = \dfrac{-.50 - (-.667)(.667)}{\sqrt{(1 - .667^2)(1 - .667^2)}} \approx \dfrac{-.50 + .444}{.556} \approx -.10\). The correlation between coffee and test score is much weaker once we account for sleep.

10

In a survey, hours of exercise per week and mood correlate at \(r = .40\). Accounting for income, the partial correlation is \(.38\). What does that suggest?

Response options

Income explains most of the link between exercise and mood

Income doesn’t explain much of the link, though other third variables still could

Exercise must be causing better mood

Income is unrelated to both exercise and mood

Hint

Compare this with what happened when we accounted for sleep in the coffee study, and with income in the spending survey.

Answer

Income doesn’t explain much of the link, though other third variables still could

Explanation

Taking income into account barely changed the correlation, so differences in income don’t appear to explain much of the link. That’s what happened with income in the spending survey. Some other third variable could still account for the link, so we still can’t conclude that exercise causes better mood.

11

A researcher has 40 people rate their stress and their sleep quality every night for a week, then calculates the correlation across all 280 pairs of ratings. Which assumption is the most obvious problem?

Response options

Independence

Bivariate normality

Linearity

Hint

The two ratings in a pair are supposed to come from the same person. What about different pairs?

Answer

Independence

Explanation

The two scores within a pair needn’t be independent, but the pairs themselves are supposed to be independent. Here, each person contributes seven pairs, and pairs from the same person will tend to resemble each other. The test would treat 280 pairs as 280 independent observations when there are really only 40 people.

12

A scatterplot shows a clear U shape: scores on \(Y\) are high when \(X\) is low, low when \(X\) is in the middle, and high again when \(X\) is high. Pearson’s \(r\) comes out close to zero. What should we conclude?

Response options

The two variables are unrelated

There’s no linear relationship, but there is a relationship that \(r\) doesn’t capture

The calculation must have gone wrong

The sample is too small to detect the relationship

Answer

There’s no linear relationship, but there is a relationship that \(r\) doesn’t capture

Explanation

Pearson’s \(r\) measures linear relationships. On one side of the U the products of deviations are mostly negative, on the other side mostly positive, and they cancel out. This is one reason to look at the scatterplot as well as the statistic.
